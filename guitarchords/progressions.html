Common Chord Progressions

How chords move together to create songs — from the three-chord trick to jazz

A chord progression is a sequence of chords that repeats to form the backbone of a song. Most songs are built on a handful of progressions that recur across genres and eras.

The Roman numerals (I, IV, V, vi…) label each chord's position in the key's major scale — uppercase = major, lowercase = minor. This notation describes the pattern independently of any specific key, so the same progression works across all keys.

Each chord name below links back to its fingering diagram on the Chord Library.

I – IV – V  The Three-Chord Trick

The most common progression in rock, country, folk, and blues. Three chords cover an enormous number of songs — it's the foundation of almost all Western popular music.

Key of C
C – F – G
Key of G
G – C – D
Key of A
A – D – E

I – V – vi – IV  The Pop Progression

Extremely common in pop music from the 1950s to today. Hundreds of hit songs use this exact pattern — it has a satisfying, uplifting quality with a brief touch of wistfulness at the vi chord.

Key of C
C – G – Am – F
Key of G
G – D – Em – C

vi – IV – I – V  Minor-Start Pop Variant

The same four chords as the pop progression, reordered to start on the minor chord. This gives a more melancholy, introspective feeling — common in pop verses before a chorus resolves on I.

Key of C
Am – F – C – G
Key of G
Em – C – G – D

I – vi – IV – V  The '50s Progression (Doo-Wop)

The classic doo-wop and early rock 'n' roll progression. It has a circular, nostalgic quality — the I chord feels like home and the sequence always returns to it. Still heard in pop ballads today.

Key of C
C – Am – F – G
Key of G
G – Em – C – D

ii – V – I  The Jazz Turnaround

The backbone of jazz harmony — also common in pop, bossa nova, and soul. Using 7th chord voicings gives this progression its sophisticated, "resolved" sound. The single most important harmonic movement in jazz.

Key of C
Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7
Key of G
Am7 – D7 – Gmaj7

I – IV – I – V  Simple Folk / Campfire Pattern

A sturdy, symmetrical loop ideal for strumming songs around a campfire. The return to I in bar three gives a reassuring, settled feeling before the V pulls you back around.

Key of D
D – G – D – A
Key of A
A – D – A – E

Transposing to other keys: Once you know a progression's Roman-numeral pattern, you can play it in any key by choosing a new I chord and counting up the major scale from there. I–IV–V in the key of D means D (I), G (IV), A (V) — the same pattern shifted up.

This is why learning the pattern matters more than memorizing chords in one key. The patterns on this page unlock a huge repertoire once you can play them in two or three keys.
