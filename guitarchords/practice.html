How to Practice

Build technique and muscle memory — whether you picked up the guitar yesterday or a month ago

Before You Start: Getting Comfortable

Good posture and hand position prevent bad habits that are hard to unlearn. Spend a minute getting these right before every session.

Posture

Sit upright with the guitar body resting against your ribs and your strumming arm draped over the body's widest point. Angle the neck slightly upward — roughly 30–45° from horizontal — so your fretting hand doesn't have to bend awkwardly. A footstool or strap both work well.

Fretting Hand

Keep your thumb behind the neck, roughly opposite your middle finger — not wrapped over the top. Arch your fingers so each fingertip lands perpendicular to the fretboard, pressing just behind the fret wire. This gives a clean, buzz-free sound with minimum pressure. If a string buzzes, press closer to the wire, or check whether an adjacent finger is touching it.

Strumming Hand

Keep your wrist relaxed and loose. Hold the pick firmly enough that it doesn't fly out on downstrokes, but not so tight that your wrist locks up. Tension is the enemy of speed and feel.

About Finger Soreness

Your fingertips will ache for the first 2–4 weeks of regular practice as calluses form — this is unavoidable and temporary. Sharp or joint pain means stop and check your technique. General fingertip soreness: fine. Pain in knuckles, wrist, or forearm: rest immediately.

The Core Practice Loop

A focused 20-minute session with this structure beats an hour of unfocused noodling for building muscle memory.

  1. Warm up (2–3 min) — Stretch your fingers gently, then walk each finger (1-2-3-4) up one string in sequence. This wakes up your hands without stressing cold tendons.
  2. Single-chord accuracy (5 min) — Fret one chord, strum, and listen: does every string ring cleanly? Pick each string individually and adjust until all sound clear. This builds the habit of checking your own sound.
  3. Chord-to-chord transitions (10–15 min) — The highest-value beginner drill. Pick two chords (e.g. G and C), get both shapes under your fingers, then practice switching between them — slowly at first, then speeding up. Most beginners can form chords but struggle to switch fast enough to keep time.
  4. Strumming with a metronome (5–10 min) — Apply the chords in rhythm, even at a very slow tempo (60–70 bpm). Timing is a separate skill from chord shaping and needs its own practice. A metronome is unforgiving in the best possible way.
  5. Apply to a song or progression (10+ min) — Use a progression from the Progressions page, or a simple song. Making practice feel purposeful helps motivation far more than abstract drilling.

Specific Tips

  • Short and often beats long and rare. 15–20 focused minutes daily builds muscle memory far faster than one 2-hour weekend session. Your brain consolidates skills during sleep — frequency matters more than total hours.
  • Use a metronome from day one. Start at 60 bpm and only increase tempo once a transition is clean at the current speed. Never chase tempo at the cost of accuracy — bad habits reinforce themselves just as much as good ones do.
  • Isolate the hard bit. When a chord change is rough, don't restart the whole song each time. Loop just those two chords for several minutes. Targeted repetition on the weak point is where improvement actually happens.
  • Notice chord relationships. E major and E minor differ by just one finger; A major and Am7 are nearly identical. When you learn a new chord, ask what it shares with one you already know — seeing these connections speeds up learning dramatically.
  • Debug buzzing one string at a time. Pick each string individually to find the buzzing one, then adjust just that finger. Most common causes: finger not close enough to the fret wire, an adjacent finger grazing a neighboring string, or not enough pressure.
  • Keep a practice log. Even a simple checklist — "Em: solid / C: shaky / G→C: improving" — shows progress that's easy to underestimate from inside the process. The Chord Library and Progressions pages can double as that checklist.

A Sample First-Month Weekly Structure

A suggested arc — move faster if a chord clicks in a few days, stay longer if it doesn't.

Week Focus
Week 1 E minor, E major, A minor — single-chord accuracy and posture. These three shapes use all four fretting fingers and are the most ergonomic starting point.
Week 2 Add C major and G major; begin two-chord switching drills (Em↔C, G↔C). C major is a stretch at first — be patient with it.
Week 3 Add D major; practice the I–IV–V and I–V–vi–IV progressions from the Progressions page at slow tempo. Focus on keeping time while switching.
Week 4 Add A major and the dominant 7ths (A7, D7, E7); try the 12-bar blues pattern in the key of A. The 7th voicings will feel surprisingly manageable by this point.

A final note on progress: learning guitar is nonlinear. Some weeks a chord that felt stuck will suddenly click; other weeks you'll feel like you're going backwards. Plateaus are a normal part of the process — not a sign to push harder or give up.

Consistency beats intensity. Showing up for 15 focused minutes today matters more than waiting until you have an hour free.
