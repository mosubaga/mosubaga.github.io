1) Fundamentals: Fourier Series & Transform

Any repeating signal can be rebuilt by stacking sine and cosine waves. A Fourier series does this for periodic signals, while a Fourier transform extends the idea to non-repeating signals by turning time into frequency.

$$f(t) = a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left(a_n\cos(2\pi n t) + b_n\sin(2\pi n t)\right)$$
Fourier series Describes periodic signals as a sum of harmonics. Each harmonic has an amplitude and phase, letting you reconstruct the original loop perfectly if you include enough terms.
Fourier transform Converts any signal into a continuous spectrum of frequencies. The result tells you how much of each frequency is present and with what phase.
Fundamental frequency The lowest frequency in the series. It sets the overall pitch or rhythm, while higher frequencies add detail and texture.
Spectrum The frequency "fingerprint" of a signal. Peaks show dominant tones, while a wide spread indicates noise or sharp changes.
$$\mathcal{F}\{f(t)\}=F(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)e^{-i\omega t}\,dt$$

3) Fourier in Machine Learning

Fourier ideas show up inside ML models whenever we want to understand patterns across scales. We can transform inputs into frequency space to spot repeating structures, or use Fourier features to help neural networks learn high-frequency details.

Fourier features

By encoding inputs with sinusoidal features, models learn sharp edges and fine details faster, which is useful in vision, graphics, and implicit neural representations.

Spectral filtering

Transforming data into the frequency domain lets models remove noise or emphasize patterns at specific scales, improving robustness.

Convolutions

Convolution in time becomes multiplication in frequency. This makes large convolutions faster and easier to analyze.

4) Fourier in Quantum Mechanics

In quantum mechanics the Fourier transform is not just a computational tool. It is built into the theory. A particle's state can be described by a wavefunction in position space, $\psi(x)$, or in momentum space, $\phi(p)$. These are two views of the same state, and the Fourier transform converts one into the other.

Waves carry momentum

The bridge is the de Broglie relation: a particle with momentum $p$ behaves like a wave with wavenumber $k = p/\hbar$. A state with one definite momentum is a pure plane wave:

$$\psi_p(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\, e^{ipx/\hbar}$$

So "which frequencies are in this wave?" becomes "which momenta does this particle have?". Any wavefunction is a superposition of these plane waves, and the weight of each one is the momentum-space wavefunction.

The position–momentum transform pair

$$\phi(p) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi(x)\, e^{-ipx/\hbar}\, dx$$ $$\psi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \phi(p)\, e^{ipx/\hbar}\, dp$$

This is the same transform as in Section 1, with time $t$ replaced by position $x$ and angular frequency $\omega$ replaced by $p/\hbar$. The symmetric $1/\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}$ factor is chosen so that probability is preserved (Plancherel's theorem):

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\psi(x)|^2\, dx = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\phi(p)|^2\, dp = 1$$
$|\psi(x)|^2$ Probability density for finding the particle at position $x$.
$|\phi(p)|^2$ Probability density for measuring momentum $p$. It is the particle's "spectrum".
Dirac notation The transform kernel is the overlap $\langle x | p \rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}} e^{ipx/\hbar}$, so $\phi(p) = \langle p | \psi \rangle$ is just a change of basis.

Derivatives become multiplication

Differentiating a plane wave just pulls down a factor: $-i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dx} e^{ipx/\hbar} = p\, e^{ipx/\hbar}$. This is why the momentum operator in position space is a derivative, while in momentum space it is plain multiplication:

$$\hat{p}\,\psi(x) = -i\hbar\,\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} \quad \overset{\mathcal{F}}{\longleftrightarrow} \quad \hat{p}\,\phi(p) = p\,\phi(p)$$ $$\hat{x}\,\psi(x) = x\,\psi(x) \quad \overset{\mathcal{F}}{\longleftrightarrow} \quad \hat{x}\,\phi(p) = i\hbar\,\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial p}$$

It is the same trick as "convolution becomes multiplication" in Section 3. Hard calculus in one space becomes simple algebra in the other.

The uncertainty principle is a Fourier fact

A signal that is short in time must have a broad frequency spectrum, and vice versa. In quantum mechanics, this bandwidth trade-off is the Heisenberg uncertainty principle. A Gaussian wave packet shows it exactly:

$$\psi(x) = \left(2\pi\sigma_x^2\right)^{-1/4} \exp\!\left(-\frac{x^2}{4\sigma_x^2} + \frac{i p_0 x}{\hbar}\right)$$ $$\phi(p) = \left(2\pi\sigma_p^2\right)^{-1/4} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(p - p_0)^2}{4\sigma_p^2}\right), \qquad \sigma_p = \frac{\hbar}{2\sigma_x}$$

The Fourier transform of a Gaussian is another Gaussian, and the widths are inversely related. Squeezing the particle into a smaller region (small $\sigma_x$) spreads its momentum (large $\sigma_p$). Gaussians are the tightest possible case, and every other state does worse:

$$\sigma_x\,\sigma_p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}$$
The uncertainty principle is not about clumsy measurements. It follows from the mathematics: no function and its Fourier transform can both be sharply concentrated. The same limit governs time and frequency in audio, which is why a very short click has no clear pitch.

Solving the Schrödinger equation

Free particle evolution

For a free particle, $\hat{H} = \hat{p}^2/2m$. In momentum space $\hat{H}$ is just multiplication by $p^2/2m$, so each plane wave only picks up a phase:

$$\phi(p, t) = \phi(p, 0)\, e^{-i p^2 t / 2m\hbar}$$ $$\psi(x, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}} \int \phi(p, 0)\, e^{i(px - p^2 t/2m)/\hbar}\, dp$$

Transform to momentum space, multiply by phases, transform back. A differential equation becomes a multiplication.

Wave packet spreading

Different momenta travel at different speeds, so the Gaussian packet above drifts at $p_0/m$ and widens over time:

$$\sigma_x(t) = \sigma_x \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{\hbar t}{2m\sigma_x^2}\right)^2}$$

The momentum distribution $|\phi(p)|^2$ never changes. Only the relative phases do, and that is what reshapes $|\psi(x)|^2$.

Split-step numerical methods

For $\hat{H} = \hat{p}^2/2m + V(x)$, simulations alternate between spaces: apply the potential in position space, FFT to momentum space, apply the kinetic phase, and inverse FFT back. The FFT makes this fast and accurate.

Where else it appears

Scattering In the Born approximation, the scattering amplitude is the Fourier transform of the potential $V(\mathbf{r})$ evaluated at the momentum transfer $\mathbf{q}$.
Crystals & band structure Periodic lattices are described in reciprocal (momentum) space. Bloch waves and X-ray diffraction patterns are Fourier pictures of the lattice.
Quantum field theory Fields are expanded in Fourier modes, and each mode $\mathbf{k}$ behaves like a harmonic oscillator whose quanta are particles.
Quantum computing The Quantum Fourier Transform is the core of Shor's factoring algorithm and of phase estimation, finding hidden periods exponentially faster than the best known classical methods.
