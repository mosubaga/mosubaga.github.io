How Basis Vectors Twist Across Curved Coordinates

Christoffel symbols, developed by Elwin Bruno Christoffel in 1869, are the "connective tissue" of tensor calculus. They track how basis vectors change from point to point, making calculus possible on curved manifolds and in general curvilinear coordinate systems.

Visual: Basis Vectors Riding a Circle

As the point travels around the circle, its radial vector $\mathbf{e}_r$ and tangential vector $\mathbf{e}_\theta$ rotate along with it. That continuous turning of the local basis is exactly what Christoffel symbols are built to measure.

1) Conceptual and Geometric Meaning

In standard Cartesian coordinates, the basis vectors $\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}$ are fixed in direction and length everywhere, so their derivatives vanish. In curvilinear coordinates (polar, cylindrical, spherical) or on curved surfaces, the basis vectors instead change direction or length from point to point.

Because the derivative of a basis vector $\mathbf{e}_i$ with respect to a coordinate $x^j$ is itself a vector, it can be written as a linear combination of the local basis vectors:

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{e}_i}{\partial x^j} = \Gamma^k_{ij} \mathbf{e}_k$$
Index $i$ Which basis vector is being differentiated.
Index $j$ Which coordinate is being varied.
Index $k$ The component direction of the resulting derivative vector.

2) First and Second Kinds

Christoffel symbols come in two forms, linked algebraically by the metric tensor.

First kind $[ij,k]$ or $\Gamma_{ij,k}$, built directly from partial derivatives of the covariant metric tensor $g_{ij}$:

$$\Gamma_{ij,k} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\partial g_{jk}}{\partial x^i} + \frac{\partial g_{ik}}{\partial x^j} - \frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial x^k} \right)$$

Second kind $\Gamma^k_{ij}$, obtained by raising the last index of the first kind with the contravariant metric tensor $g^{km}$:

$$\Gamma^k_{ij} = g^{km} \Gamma_{ij,m} = \frac{1}{2} g^{km} \left( \frac{\partial g_{jm}}{\partial x^i} + \frac{\partial g_{im}}{\partial x^j} - \frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial x^m} \right)$$

Symmetry

In standard Riemannian geometry (torsion-free space), the lower indices are symmetric: $\Gamma^k_{ij} = \Gamma^k_{ji}$.

Vanishing in flat space

In rectangular Cartesian coordinates, where the metric components are constant, all Christoffel symbols vanish: $\Gamma^k_{ij} = 0$.

3) Non-Tensorial Nature

Despite having indices, Christoffel symbols are not tensors. Under a general change of coordinates from $x$ to $\bar{x}$, their transformation law carries an extra non-linear term:

$$\bar{\Gamma}^k_{ij} = \Gamma^r_{pq} \frac{\partial x^p}{\partial \bar{x}^i} \frac{\partial x^q}{\partial \bar{x}^j} \frac{\partial \bar{x}^k}{\partial x^r} + \frac{\partial^2 x^r}{\partial \bar{x}^i \partial \bar{x}^j} \frac{\partial \bar{x}^k}{\partial x^r}$$
The second-derivative term spoils the standard tensor transformation property. This reflects how Christoffel symbols capture coordinate-dependent inertial artifacts, like centrifugal and Coriolis effects, rather than intrinsic tensor fields alone. Under purely linear coordinate transformations the second derivative vanishes, and they do transform like tensors.

4) Worked Example: Cartesian to Polar Coordinates

The flat plane is the simplest place to watch Christoffel symbols appear. In Cartesian coordinates $(x, y)$ they are all zero, yet in polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ describing the same flat plane, some of them are not. Here we compute them three ways and then put them to work.

Step 1: The coordinate transformation

$$x = r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta$$ $$r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}, \qquad \theta = \operatorname{atan2}(y, x)$$

The position vector is $\mathbf{R} = r\cos\theta\,\mathbf{i} + r\sin\theta\,\mathbf{j}$. The natural (coordinate) basis vectors are its partial derivatives:

$$\mathbf{e}_r = \frac{\partial \mathbf{R}}{\partial r} = \cos\theta\,\mathbf{i} + \sin\theta\,\mathbf{j}, \qquad \mathbf{e}_\theta = \frac{\partial \mathbf{R}}{\partial \theta} = -r\sin\theta\,\mathbf{i} + r\cos\theta\,\mathbf{j}$$

Unlike $\mathbf{i}$ and $\mathbf{j}$, these change direction with $\theta$, and $\mathbf{e}_\theta$ also grows in length with $r$.

Step 2: The metric tensor

Dot products of the basis vectors give the metric, equivalently the line element $ds^2 = dx^2 + dy^2$ rewritten in polar form:

$$g_{ij} = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{e}_r\cdot\mathbf{e}_r & \mathbf{e}_r\cdot\mathbf{e}_\theta \\ \mathbf{e}_\theta\cdot\mathbf{e}_r & \mathbf{e}_\theta\cdot\mathbf{e}_\theta \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & r^2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad g^{ij} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/r^2 \end{pmatrix}$$ $$ds^2 = dr^2 + r^2\,d\theta^2$$

Step 3a: Christoffel symbols from differentiating the basis

Apply the defining relation $\partial_j \mathbf{e}_i = \Gamma^k_{ij}\mathbf{e}_k$ directly:

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{e}_r}{\partial r} = \mathbf{0}$$ $$\frac{\partial \mathbf{e}_r}{\partial \theta} = -\sin\theta\,\mathbf{i} + \cos\theta\,\mathbf{j} = \frac{1}{r}\,\mathbf{e}_\theta$$ $$\frac{\partial \mathbf{e}_\theta}{\partial r} = -\sin\theta\,\mathbf{i} + \cos\theta\,\mathbf{j} = \frac{1}{r}\,\mathbf{e}_\theta$$ $$\frac{\partial \mathbf{e}_\theta}{\partial \theta} = -r\cos\theta\,\mathbf{i} - r\sin\theta\,\mathbf{j} = -r\,\mathbf{e}_r$$

Reading off the coefficients:

$\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = -r$ Moving in $\theta$ turns $\mathbf{e}_\theta$ inward, toward the center.
$\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta} = \Gamma^\theta_{\theta r} = \dfrac{1}{r}$ $\mathbf{e}_\theta$ stretches with $r$, and $\mathbf{e}_r$ rotates with $\theta$.
All others $= 0$ $\Gamma^r_{rr} = \Gamma^r_{r\theta} = \Gamma^\theta_{rr} = \Gamma^\theta_{\theta\theta} = 0$.

Step 3b: The same result from the metric

Only $g_{\theta\theta} = r^2$ depends on a coordinate, and only through $\partial_r g_{\theta\theta} = 2r$. Plugging into the second-kind formula:

$$\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = \tfrac{1}{2} g^{rr}\left( \partial_\theta g_{\theta r} + \partial_\theta g_{\theta r} - \partial_r g_{\theta\theta} \right) = \tfrac{1}{2}(1)(0 + 0 - 2r) = -r$$ $$\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta} = \tfrac{1}{2} g^{\theta\theta}\left( \partial_r g_{\theta\theta} + \partial_\theta g_{r\theta} - \partial_\theta g_{r\theta} \right) = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{r^2}\cdot 2r = \frac{1}{r}$$

Step 3c: The same result from the transformation law

Take the unbarred coordinates to be Cartesian $(x, y)$, where $\Gamma = 0$, and the barred coordinates to be $(r, \theta)$. The tensorial term in the transformation law drops out, and only the inhomogeneous second-derivative term survives:

$$\bar{\Gamma}^k_{ij} = \underbrace{0}_{\text{Cartesian}} + \frac{\partial^2 x^m}{\partial \bar{x}^i\,\partial \bar{x}^j}\,\frac{\partial \bar{x}^k}{\partial x^m}$$

The ingredients are the inverse Jacobian and the second derivatives of $x(r,\theta)$, $y(r,\theta)$:

$$\frac{\partial r}{\partial x} = \cos\theta, \quad \frac{\partial r}{\partial y} = \sin\theta, \quad \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial x} = -\frac{\sin\theta}{r}, \quad \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial y} = \frac{\cos\theta}{r}$$ $$\frac{\partial^2 (x, y)}{\partial \theta^2} = (-r\cos\theta,\ -r\sin\theta), \qquad \frac{\partial^2 (x, y)}{\partial r\,\partial\theta} = (-\sin\theta,\ \cos\theta), \qquad \frac{\partial^2 (x, y)}{\partial r^2} = (0,\ 0)$$

Contracting them:

$$\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = (-r\cos\theta)(\cos\theta) + (-r\sin\theta)(\sin\theta) = -r$$ $$\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta} = (-\sin\theta)\left(-\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\right) + (\cos\theta)\left(\frac{\cos\theta}{r}\right) = \frac{1}{r}$$ $$\Gamma^r_{r\theta} = (-\sin\theta)(\cos\theta) + (\cos\theta)(\sin\theta) = 0, \qquad \Gamma^\theta_{\theta\theta} = (-r\cos\theta)\left(-\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\right) + (-r\sin\theta)\left(\frac{\cos\theta}{r}\right) = 0$$
This is the non-tensorial nature from Section 3 in action. A genuine tensor that is zero in one coordinate system is zero in every coordinate system. Christoffel symbols are zero in Cartesian coordinates but not in polar coordinates, purely because the transformation $x = r\cos\theta,\ y = r\sin\theta$ is nonlinear. The plane itself has not become curved.

Step 4: Using the symbols

Straight lines (geodesics)

Inserting the polar symbols into the geodesic equation gives two equations:

$$\ddot{r} - r\,\dot{\theta}^2 = 0$$ $$\ddot{\theta} + \frac{2}{r}\,\dot{r}\,\dot{\theta} = 0$$

Their solutions are exactly the straight lines $r\cos(\theta - \theta_0) = d$. In Cartesian coordinates the same lines are simply $\ddot{x} = \ddot{y} = 0$.

Centrifugal and Coriolis terms

For any moving particle, the acceleration written in the unit polar basis is

$$\mathbf{a} = \left(\ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2\right)\hat{\mathbf{r}} + \left(r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}\right)\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$$

The $-r\dot{\theta}^2$ term comes from $\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta}$ (centripetal) and the $2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}$ term from $\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta}$ (Coriolis). They are artifacts of the coordinates, not real forces. Setting $\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}$ gives back the geodesic equations.

Divergence

Contracting the covariant derivative gives $\nabla_i V^i = \partial_i V^i + \Gamma^i_{ik} V^k$, with $\Gamma^i_{ir} = \Gamma^r_{rr} + \Gamma^\theta_{\theta r} = 1/r$ and $\Gamma^i_{i\theta} = 0$:

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{V} = \frac{\partial V^r}{\partial r} + \frac{\partial V^\theta}{\partial \theta} + \frac{V^r}{r}$$

The extra $V^r/r$ accounts for the radial lines spreading apart.

Laplacian

For a scalar $f$, $\nabla^2 f = g^{ij}\left(\partial_i\partial_j f - \Gamma^k_{ij}\,\partial_k f\right)$. Only $\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = -r$ contributes the correction:

$$\nabla^2 f = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial r^2} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial f}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial \theta^2}$$

This recovers the familiar polar Laplacian. The $\frac{1}{r}\partial_r f$ term comes entirely from the Christoffel symbol.

$\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = -r$ $\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta} = 1/r$ Cartesian: all $\Gamma = 0$ Curvature $R = 0$: still flat

5) Major Applications in Tensor Calculus

Christoffel symbols are the connective glue behind several core constructions in differential geometry and physics.

A. Covariant differentiation

An ordinary partial derivative of a vector's components, $\partial_j V^i$, ignores how the basis vectors themselves vary. The covariant derivative corrects for this:

$$\nabla_j V^i = \frac{\partial V^i}{\partial x^j} + \Gamma^i_{kj} V^k$$ $$\nabla_j V_i = \frac{\partial V_i}{\partial x^j} - \Gamma^k_{ij} V_k$$

This keeps the derivative of a tensor a valid tensor across every coordinate system.

B. Geodesics and motion

The "straightest possible path" between two points on a curved space is a geodesic, governed by:

$$\frac{d^2 x^k}{ds^2} + \Gamma^k_{ij} \frac{dx^i}{ds} \frac{dx^j}{ds} = 0$$

In General Relativity this describes a freely falling particle, with gravity interpreted as spacetime curvature encoded by Christoffel symbols.

C. Riemann curvature tensor

To test whether a space is intrinsically curved, build the Riemann-Christoffel curvature tensor from the symbols and their partial derivatives:

$$R^\sigma_{\alpha\beta\gamma} = \frac{\partial \Gamma^\sigma_{\alpha\gamma}}{\partial x^\beta} - \frac{\partial \Gamma^\sigma_{\alpha\beta}}{\partial x^\gamma} + \Gamma^\tau_{\alpha\gamma} \Gamma^\sigma_{\tau\beta} - \Gamma^\tau_{\alpha\beta} \Gamma^\sigma_{\tau\gamma}$$

If every component vanishes everywhere, the space is flat and a coordinate system exists where all Christoffel symbols vanish too.

D. Curvilinear differential operators

Christoffel symbols let classical operators such as divergence, curl, and the Laplacian be rigorously formulated in non-Cartesian systems, like spherical or cylindrical polar coordinates, while preserving coordinate invariance.
