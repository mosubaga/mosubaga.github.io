Peking Duck: An Imperial Feast

October 26, 2026

Imperial Origins

Peking Duck (北京烤鸭) has been a prized dish since the Yuan Dynasty (1271–1368), when it appeared in imperial court menus. It reached its pinnacle during the Ming and Qing Dynasties, when it became the centerpiece of state banquets and diplomatic feasts. The dish is so important to Chinese culinary heritage that it is often called China s national dish.

The most famous Peking duck restaurant, Quanjude, was founded in Beijing in 1864 and has served the dish continuously for over 160 years. Their ovens have roasted hundreds of millions of ducks, and the technique has been passed down through generations of roast masters.

The Art of Preparation

Making authentic Peking duck is a multi-day process. The duck is inflated to separate the skin from the fat, glazed with a maltose syrup, and air-dried for 24 to 48 hours. It is then roasted in a specialized oven using fruitwood (traditionally jujube, peach, or pear wood), which imparts a subtle sweetness to the smoke.

The result is extraordinary: skin so crispy it shatters like glass, a thin layer of rendered fat, and tender, juicy meat beneath. A skilled carver will produce over 100 thin slices from a single duck, each piece containing skin, fat, and meat in perfect proportion.

The Ritual of Eating

Peking duck is served in courses. The prized skin comes first, dipped in sugar. Then sliced duck is wrapped in thin wheat pancakes with hoisin sauce, scallion, and cucumber. Finally, the carcass is used to make a rich soup. Nothing is wasted — a philosophy deeply embedded in Chinese cooking.

Peking Duck is not merely a dish but a ceremony — proof that the greatest cooking transforms patience and precision into something transcendent.
