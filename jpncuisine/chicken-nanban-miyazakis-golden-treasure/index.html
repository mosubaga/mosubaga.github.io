Chicken Nanban: Miyazaki’s Golden Treasure

October 26, 2026

Born in Miyazaki

Chicken Nanban (チキン南蛮) was created in the 1960s in Nobeoka, Miyazaki Prefecture, on the southern island of Kyushu. The dish is credited to chefs who had trained at a Western-style restaurant called Rogen, where a simple dish of fried chicken marinated in vinegar — inspired by the Portuguese-influenced nanban (南蛮, “southern barbarian”) cooking tradition — was a staff meal favorite.

Two former Rogen employees went on to open their own restaurants and refined the dish in different directions. One version, from the restaurant Naganoya, added the now-iconic tartar sauce topping, creating the style that became famous nationwide. The other, from Ogura, kept it closer to the original vinegar-marinated style. Both restaurants still operate in Nobeoka today, and locals debate passionately over which is the “real” chicken nanban.

The Nanban Connection

The word nanban (南蛮) has a fascinating history in Japanese cuisine. It originally referred to the Portuguese and Spanish traders who arrived in southern Japan in the 16th century. These “southern barbarians” brought with them new ingredients and cooking techniques, including deep-frying (which influenced tempura) and the use of vinegar and chili in cooking.

Nanban-style cooking in Japan typically involves marinating fried food in a sweet vinegar sauce — a technique seen in dishes like aji no nanban (fried horse mackerel in vinegar) and negi nanban (soba with duck and leek). Chicken nanban is the most famous descendant of this tradition.

The Dish

Making chicken nanban is a three-step process, each layer building on the last:

  1. The chicken — boneless thigh or breast is coated in flour and beaten egg, then deep-fried until golden and crispy.
  2. The nanban sauce — immediately after frying, the hot chicken is plunged into a sweet-and-sour sauce made from soy sauce, rice vinegar, sugar, and sometimes dashi. The crispy coating absorbs the tangy sauce while staying slightly crunchy.
  3. The tartar sauce — a generous dollop of rich, chunky tartar sauce made with boiled egg, onion, pickles, and mayonnaise is piled on top. The cool, creamy sauce against the hot, tangy chicken is what makes the dish irresistible.

From Local Specialty to National Favorite

For decades, chicken nanban was known only in Miyazaki. It began appearing on menus across Japan in the 2000s, boosted by regional food tourism and TV coverage. Today it is served in convenience stores, family restaurants, and bento shops nationwide, and is consistently ranked among the most popular yoshoku dishes in surveys.

In Miyazaki, chicken nanban is a source of deep regional pride. It is the city of Nobeoka s official promotional dish, featured in local festivals, and served in school lunches. Visiting Miyazaki without eating chicken nanban is like visiting Naples without eating pizza.

Chicken nanban is the story of Miyazaki in a single dish — local pride, Portuguese echoes, and the simple genius of combining crispy, tangy, and creamy on one plate.
