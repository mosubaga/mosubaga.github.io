Differential Forms for Physics

A worked guide from multivariable calculus to electromagnetism, mechanics, thermodynamics, fluids, gauge theory, and general relativity. This guide consolidates Physics with Differential Forms and Doing Physics with Differential Forms and develops the calculations behind their summaries.

You can start with multivariable calculus and linear algebra. If you have studied Christoffel symbols in polar or spherical coordinates, §9 shows how to obtain the same geometric information from an orthonormal coframe. If you have studied the Legendre transform in mechanics, §7 shows its thermodynamic counterpart.

1. Covectors, forms, and the wedge product

A smooth manifold is a space with local coordinate charts whose overlaps have smooth coordinate changes. At a point, tangent vectors describe velocities of curves. Covectors are linear maps from tangent vectors to numbers. A differential \(k\)-form smoothly assigns to each point an alternating multilinear function of \(k\) tangent vectors. Interchanging two inputs changes the sign. Repeated inputs give zero.

Degree Typical local expression Physical interpretation
0 \(f\) Temperature or potential
1 \(a\,dx+b\,dy+c\,dz\) Work along a displacement
2 \(a\,dy\wedge dz+b\,dz\wedge dx+c\,dx\wedge dy\) Flux through an oriented surface
3 \(f\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz\) Amount per oriented volume

In coordinates \(x^i\), \(dx^i(v)=v^i\). The \(\binom nk\) wedges with strictly increasing indices form a basis of \(k\)-forms in \(n\) dimensions. If you sum over all ordered indices, write \(\omega=\frac1{k!}\omega_{i_1\cdots i_k}dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_k}\), with alternating components. The wedge of a \(k\)-form and an \(\ell\)-form satisfies \(\alpha\wedge\beta=(-1)^{k\ell}\beta\wedge\alpha\).

Worked example 1 — Directional change without a dot product

Let \(T(x,y)=x^2+3y\). At \((2,1)\), \(dT=4dx+3dy\). For the velocity \(v=2\partial_x-\partial_y\), \(dT(v)=8-3=5\). If \(v=\dot\gamma(0)\), the chain rule gives \(\frac{d}{dt}T(\gamma(t))|_0=5\). A metric would let you represent this covector by a gradient vector, but the directional derivative itself requires only the curve and the function.

Worked example 2 — Wedges measure signed projected area

For covectors \(\alpha,\beta\),

\[ (\alpha\wedge\beta)(u,v)=\alpha(u)\beta(v)-\alpha(v)\beta(u). \]

For \(u=(2,1)\) and \(v=(1,3)\), \((dx\wedge dy)(u,v)=6-1=5\). Reversing the vectors gives \(-5\). Also \((2dx+dy)\wedge(dx-3dy)=-7dx\wedge dy\). In four dimensions a 2-form can have a nonzero wedge square. For example \((dx\wedge dy+dz\wedge dw)^2=2dx\wedge dy\wedge dz\wedge dw\), where the square denotes a wedge product.

2. Exterior derivative and topology

Compute \(d\) in a coordinate basis by differentiating coefficients and wedging the resulting differential on the left.

\[ d(f\,dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_k})=df\wedge dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_k}. \]

For arbitrary forms use \(d(\alpha\wedge\beta)=d\alpha\wedge\beta+(-1)^k\alpha\wedge d\beta\) when \(\alpha\) has degree \(k\). In particular, if your basis consists of moving coframe forms, you must also differentiate the basis.

Worked example 3 — Exterior derivative of a circulation form

Take \(\alpha=x^2y\,dx+xy^2\,dy\). The \(dx\wedge dx\) and \(dy\wedge dy\) terms vanish. The remaining terms give

\[ d\alpha=x^2\,dy\wedge dx+y^2\,dx\wedge dy=(y^2-x^2)\,dx\wedge dy. \]

For a smooth function \(f\), \(d(df)=\sum_{i,j}\partial_j\partial_i f\,dx^j\wedge dx^i=0\). Mixed partials are symmetric and wedge factors are antisymmetric. The same cancellation on coefficients gives \(d^2=0\) on every smooth form.

Worked example 4 — A locally conservative field with nonzero circulation

A form is closed if \(d\omega=0\) and exact if \(\omega=d\eta\). Exact forms are closed. On a star-shaped open set, the Poincaré lemma gives the converse for positive-degree forms. On the punctured plane consider

\[ \eta=\frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}. \]

On a polar coordinate patch \(\eta=d\theta\), hence \(d\eta=0\). Along the counterclockwise unit circle, \(\eta\) pulls back to \(dt\) for \(0\le t\le2\pi\). Its integral is \(2\pi\). An exact 1-form has zero integral around a closed loop by the fundamental theorem of calculus, so this form has no global single-valued primitive. The circle cannot be filled by a disk lying entirely within the punctured plane.

3. Pullback, integration, and Stokes

For a map \(\Phi:N\to M\), pull back a form by evaluating it on pushed forward tangent vectors. In coordinates, substitute \(x^i=\Phi^i(u)\) and replace \(dx^i\) by \(d\Phi^i\). Pullback respects wedges and satisfies \(\Phi^*(d\omega)=d(\Phi^*\omega)\). To integrate a \(k\)-form over a parametrized oriented \(k\)-dimensional region, integrate the coefficient of the ordered parameter wedge after pulling back.

Worked example 5 — Polar Jacobian from a wedge

For \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\),

\[ dx=\cos\theta\,dr-r\sin\theta\,d\theta,\qquad dy=\sin\theta\,dr+r\cos\theta\,d\theta. \]

Wedging gives \(dx\wedge dy=r\,dr\wedge d\theta\). The area of a disk of radius \(R\) is therefore \(\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^R r\,dr\,d\theta=\pi R^2\). The polar singularity at the origin can be handled by integrating over annuli and letting the inner radius approach zero.

Worked example 6 — Work along a path

Let \(\alpha=y\,dx+x\,dy\). Along \(\gamma(t)=(t,t^2)\), \(0\le t\le1\), \(\gamma^*\alpha=(t^2+2t^2)dt=3t^2dt\). The work is 1. Since \(\alpha=d(xy)\), every smooth path from \((0,0)\) to \((1,1)\) gives the same value.

Worked example 7 — Flux through a tilted surface

For the constant field \(B=(0,0,2)\) the flux form is \(2dx\wedge dy\). On the upward-oriented graph \((u,v,u+2v)\) over the unit square it pulls back to \(2du\wedge dv\), giving flux 2. In vector language the oriented area vector is \((-1,-2,1)\,du\,dv\). Its dot product with \(B\) is 2. The horizontal projection accounts for the tilt automatically.

Worked example 8 — Stokes on a disk

For a compact oriented \(k\)-manifold \(K\) with boundary,

\[ \int_K d\omega=\int_{\partial K}\omega,\qquad \deg\omega=k-1. \]

Use the outward-first convention for the boundary orientation. For the positively oriented disk, take \(\omega=(x\,dy-y\,dx)/2\). Then \(d\omega=dx\wedge dy\). On the counterclockwise boundary of radius \(R\), \(\omega=R^2d\theta/2\). Both integrals are \(\pi R^2\).

\(K\) \(\omega\) Familiar theorem
Interval Function Fundamental theorem of calculus
Plane region Circulation 1-form Green's circulation theorem
Surface in space Circulation 1-form Classical Stokes theorem
Solid region Flux 2-form Divergence theorem

4. Metrics, Hodge star, and vector calculus

A metric \(g\) converts a vector \(X\) into the covector \(X^\flat=g(X,\cdot)\). The inverse operation is denoted \(\sharp\). With an orientation, \(g\) also defines the Hodge star by

\[ \alpha\wedge *\beta=\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle_g\,\mathrm{vol}_g \]

for forms of equal degree. In a positive orthonormal coframe, take the complementary basis wedge with the sign that makes the ordered product positive. In Lorentzian signature, also multiply by the metric norm squared of the starting basis wedge. In dimension \(n\) with \(s\) negative directions, \(**\alpha=(-1)^{k(n-k)+s}\alpha\) on \(k\)-forms.

Contraction inserts a vector in the first slot. In particular,

\[ \iota_X(\alpha\wedge\beta)=(\iota_X\alpha)\wedge\beta+(-1)^k\alpha\wedge\iota_X\beta. \]

In Euclidean 3-space, \(\iota_X\mathrm{vol}=*X^\flat\). The same vector field can therefore be encoded as a circulation 1-form or a flux 2-form.

Operation Forms in oriented Euclidean 3-space
Gradient \((\operatorname{grad} f)^\flat=df\)
Curl \((\operatorname{curl}X)^\flat=*dX^\flat\)
Divergence \(d(\iota_X\mathrm{vol})=(\operatorname{div}X)\mathrm{vol}\)

Worked example 9 — Curl and divergence in Cartesian coordinates

For \(X=(-y,x,0)\), \(X^\flat=-y\,dx+x\,dy\). Then \(dX^\flat=2dx\wedge dy\), so \(\operatorname{curl}X=2\partial_z\). The flux form is \(-y\,dy\wedge dz+x\,dz\wedge dx\), whose derivative vanishes. Applying \(d^2=0\) to \(f\) and \(X^\flat\) explains \(\operatorname{curl}\operatorname{grad}f=0\) and \(\operatorname{div}\operatorname{curl}X=0\).

Worked example 10 — Why spherical divergence has an \(r^2\)

For \(r>0\) and away from polar coordinate singularities, the orthonormal coframe is \((dr,r\,d\vartheta,r\sin\vartheta\,d\varphi)\). Its volume form is \(r^2\sin\vartheta\,dr\wedge d\vartheta\wedge d\varphi\). For \(X=f(r)\partial_r\) the flux form is \(f(r)r^2\sin\vartheta\,d\vartheta\wedge d\varphi\). Taking \(d\) and dividing by the volume coefficient gives

\[ \operatorname{div}X=\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}(r^2f(r)). \]

For \(f=C/r^2\) the divergence vanishes at every point of the punctured space, yet the flux through any outward sphere is \(4\pi C\). A shell has two boundary spheres with opposite induced orientations, so its total boundary flux is zero. To apply the divergence theorem to a full ball, one must account for the singularity at the origin.

5. Electromagnetism

Use flat spacetime with coordinates \((t,x,y,z)\), metric \(-dt^2+dx^2+dy^2+dz^2\), orientation \(dt\wedge dx\wedge dy\wedge dz\), and units \(c=\epsilon_0=\mu_0=1\). Denote the spatial exterior derivative and Hodge star by \(d_s\) and \(*_3\). Let \(e=E^\flat\), \(b=*_3 B^\flat\), \(j=*_3\mathbf J^\flat\), and \(v_3=dx\wedge dy\wedge dz\). Define

\[ F=b-dt\wedge e,\qquad \mathcal J=\rho v_3-dt\wedge j,\qquad dF=0,\quad d(*F)=\mathcal J. \]

Here \(\mathcal J\) is the current 3-form, so the degrees of the source equation agree. For the current vector \(J=\rho\partial_t+\mathbf J\), \(\mathcal J=\iota_J\mathrm{vol}_4=*J^\flat\). Applying a Hodge star to both sides gives \(*d(*F)=**J^\flat=J^\flat\) with the stated signature. Writing the source equation directly with the current 3-form avoids ambiguity when changing conventions.

Worked example 11 — Recover all four Maxwell equations

The spacetime star gives \(*F=*_3e+dt\wedge B^\flat\). For instance \(*(dt\wedge dx)=-dy\wedge dz\) and \(*(dy\wedge dz)=dt\wedge dx\). Now compute

\[ dF=d_sb+dt\wedge(\partial_t b+d_se), \]

\[ d(*F)=d_s(*_3e)+dt\wedge(\partial_t(*_3e)-d_sB^\flat). \]

Match spatial and time-containing terms.

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla\cdot B&=0,& \partial_t B+\nabla\times E&=0,\\ \nabla\cdot E&=\rho,& \nabla\times B-\partial_t E&=\mathbf J. \end{aligned} \]

The first pair uses only \(d\) on \(F\). The second pair uses the metric through \(*\). On curved spacetime the same form equations apply using the spacetime metric and orientation to define the star.

Worked example 12 — Potentials and gauge freedom

Locally \(dF=0\) implies \(F=dA\). Put \(A=a-\phi\,dt\), with \(a\) the spatial vector-potential 1-form. Then

\[ dA=d_sa+dt\wedge(\partial_t a+d_s\phi). \]

Thus \(B^\flat=*_3d_sa\) and \(e=-\partial_t a-d_s\phi\). Under \(A\mapsto A+d\chi\), \(a\mapsto a+d_s\chi\) and \(\phi\mapsto\phi-\partial_t\chi\). The field stays the same because \(d^2\chi=0\).

For a uniform magnetic field \(B_0\partial_z\), choose \(a=\frac{B_0}{2}(x\,dy-y\,dx)\) and \(\phi=0\). Then \(F=B_0dx\wedge dy\). Another choice is \(a'=B_0x\,dy\). Their difference is \(d(B_0xy/2)\).

Worked example 13 — A plane wave and its dual

Set \(u=t-z\) and \(A=a(u)\,dx\). Then

\[ F=a'(u)(dt-dz)\wedge dx,\qquad *F=a'(u)(dz-dt)\wedge dy. \]

Both exterior derivatives vanish because \(da'=a''(u)du\) and \(du\wedge du=0\). The fields are \(E=-a'\partial_x\) and \(B=-a'\partial_y\). Their cross product points along \(+\partial_z\), the direction in which a fixed phase \(t-z\) propagates.

Worked example 14 — Faraday induction on a stationary circle

Take \(B=B(t)\partial_z\) and an oriented disk of radius \(R\) with normal \(+\partial_z\). The equation \(\partial_t b=-d_s e\) and Stokes give

\[ \oint_{\partial D} e=-\frac{d}{dt}\int_D b=-\pi R^2\dot B. \]

For an azimuthally symmetric electric field, \(E_\theta(R)=-R\dot B/2\). A compatible local potential is \(a=B(t)(x\,dy-y\,dx)/2\) with \(\phi=0\). This illustrates the homogeneous equations. Whether particular time dependence is supported by sources is determined by the inhomogeneous equations.

Worked example 15 — Charge conservation and the Coulomb field

Apply \(d\) to \(d(*F)=\mathcal J\) to obtain \(d\mathcal J=0\). In components,

\[ d\mathcal J=dt\wedge(\partial_t\rho\,v_3+d_sj), \]

which is the continuity equation. Integrating over a fixed spatial region gives the rate of change of its charge as minus the outward current flux.

For a static point charge \(q\), use \(\phi=q/(4\pi r)\) away from the origin. Then \(e=-d_s\phi=q\,dr/(4\pi r^2)\) and \(*_3e=(q/4\pi)\sin\vartheta\,d\vartheta\wedge d\varphi\). Its outward sphere integral is \(q\). The source vanishes in the punctured domain. On the full domain the source is a delta distribution at the origin.

6. Hamiltonian mechanics

A system with configuration manifold \(Q\) has canonical phase space \(T^*Q\). Its coordinates \((q^i,p_i)\) pair positions with covector components of momentum. The canonical 1-form and symplectic 2-form are

\[ \theta=p_i\,dq^i,\qquad \omega=-d\theta=\sum_i dq^i\wedge dp_i. \]

A symplectic form is closed and nondegenerate. Nondegeneracy makes \(X_H\) unique in \(\iota_{X_H}\omega=dH\). For \(X_H=\dot q^i\partial_{q^i}+\dot p_i\partial_{p_i}\), contraction yields \(\dot q^i dp_i-\dot p_i dq^i\). Comparing coefficients gives Hamilton's equations.

Worked example 16 — Harmonic oscillator and phase-space area

For \(H=p^2/(2m)+m\nu^2q^2/2\),

\[ X_H=(p/m)\partial_q-m\nu^2 q\,\partial_p. \]

Thus \(\ddot q=-\nu^2 q\). A solution is \(q=A\cos(\nu t+\delta)\) and \(p=-m\nu A\sin(\nu t+\delta)\). Energy conservation follows immediately from \(X_H(H)=\omega(X_H,X_H)=0\). Cartan's formula for the Lie derivative,

\[ \mathcal L_X\alpha=d(\iota_X\alpha)+\iota_Xd\alpha, \]

gives \(\mathcal L_{X_H}\omega=d^2H=0\). The flow preserves \(\omega\) and hence the phase-space volume \(\omega^{\wedge n}/n!\). For one oscillator the area inside the energy ellipse \(H\le E\) is \(\pi\sqrt{2E/(m\nu^2)}\sqrt{2mE}=2\pi E/\nu\).

Worked example 17 — Polar coordinates and canonical momenta

In the plane, substitute \(x=r\cos\theta\) and \(y=r\sin\theta\) in \(p_xdx+p_ydy\). Matching coefficients of \(dr\) and \(d\theta\) gives

\[ p_r=p_x\cos\theta+p_y\sin\theta,\qquad p_\theta=r(-p_x\sin\theta+p_y\cos\theta)=xp_y-yp_x. \]

Because the 1-form is preserved, its negative exterior derivative is also preserved. For a central potential \(V(r)\),

\[ H=\frac{p_r^2}{2m}+\frac{p_\theta^2}{2mr^2}+V(r). \]

Hamilton's equations give \(\dot p_\theta=0\), \(\dot\theta=p_\theta/(mr^2)\), and \(\dot p_r=p_\theta^2/(mr^3)-V'(r)\). The angular momentum is precisely the coefficient of \(d\theta\) in the canonical 1-form.

7. Thermodynamics

For a simple compressible system at fixed particle number, take equilibrium coordinates \((S,V)\) and a smooth energy \(U(S,V)\). With \(T=U_S\) and \(P=-U_V\), \(dU=T\,dS-P\,dV\). Along a reversible process the heat supplied is \(\delta Q_{\rm rev}=T\,dS\) and the work done by the system is \(\delta W_{\rm rev}=P\,dV\). For irreversible processes, \(T\,dS\) is generally different from the actual heat supplied.

Worked example 18 — Maxwell relations by differentiating twice

From \(d^2U=0\),

\[ 0=dT\wedge dS-dP\wedge dV=-(T_V+P_S)dS\wedge dV. \]

Hence \((\partial T/\partial V)_S=-(\partial P/\partial S)_V\). With \(F_{\rm th}=U-TS\), the product rule gives \(dF_{\rm th}=-S\,dT-P\,dV\). In a region where \((T,V)\) are valid equilibrium coordinates, \(d^2F_{\rm th}=0\) yields \((\partial S/\partial V)_T=(\partial P/\partial T)_V\). For an ideal gas with \(P=nRT/V\), this gives \((\partial S/\partial V)_T=nR/V\) and the isothermal entropy change \(nR\log(V_2/V_1)\).

Worked example 19 — A loop with nonzero work

For a reversible ideal-gas cycle in \((T,V)\), expand isothermally from \(V_1\) to \(V_2\) at \(T_h\), cool at fixed volume, compress at \(T_c\), and heat at fixed volume. The isochoric legs give zero work. The other legs give

\[ \oint P\,dV=nR(T_h-T_c)\log(V_2/V_1). \]

The same result follows from \(d(P\,dV)=(nR/V)dT\wedge dV\). The path has the boundary orientation of the positive \(dT\wedge dV\) rectangle. The total energy change is zero because \(dU\) is exact. For reversible heat, \(\delta Q_{\rm rev}/T=dS\) is also exact. Temperature supplies an integrating factor \(1/T\).

Worked example 20 — Contact form and a change of potential

On the five-dimensional space \((U,S,V,T,P)\) let \(\lambda=dU-T\,dS+P\,dV\). Since \(d\lambda=-dT\wedge dS+dP\wedge dV\), the 5-form \(\lambda\wedge(d\lambda)^{\wedge2}\) is nowhere zero. This is the contact condition. The equilibrium surface pulls \(\lambda\) back to zero by the first law. For enthalpy \(H_{\rm th}=U+PV\), substitution gives \(\lambda=dH_{\rm th}-T\,dS-V\,dP\). On the same equilibrium surface this reads \(dH_{\rm th}=T\,dS+V\,dP\).

8. Fluid mechanics and conservation

On a fixed oriented spatial region with volume form \(v_3\), mass density is a 3-form \(m=\rho v_3\). For flow velocity \(u\), the mass crossing a fixed oriented surface per unit time is represented by \(\iota_um\). Mass conservation reads

\[ \partial_t m+d(\iota_um)=0. \]

It is equivalent to \(\partial_t m+\mathcal L_um=0\) since \(dm=0\) on a three-dimensional spatial manifold.

Worked example 21 — Expanding flow

For \(u=a(x\partial_x+y\partial_y+z\partial_z)\) and spatially uniform initial density \(\rho_0\), the continuity equation reduces to \(\dot\rho+3a\rho=0\). Thus \(\rho(t)=\rho_0e^{-3at}\). A region moving with this flow scales by \(e^{at}\) in each direction, so its volume scales by \(e^{3at}\). Its total mass stays constant.

Worked example 22 — Vorticity and circulation

For rigid planar rotation \(u=\Omega(-y\partial_x+x\partial_y)\), the velocity 1-form is \(u^\flat=\Omega(-y\,dx+x\,dy)\). The vorticity 2-form is \(du^\flat=2\Omega\,dx\wedge dy\). Stokes gives \(\oint u^\flat=2\Omega\pi R^2\) around a counterclockwise circle of radius \(R\). Directly, its tangential speed \(\Omega R\) multiplied by the circumference gives the same result.

9. Cartan geometry and general relativity

Choose a local orthonormal coframe \(e^a\) with dual frame \(E_a\). In GR use \(g=\eta_{ab}e^a\otimes e^b\) with \(\eta=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,1,1)\). The connection forms are defined by \(\nabla E_b=\omega^a{}_b\otimes E_a\) in the sense that \(\nabla_XE_b=\omega^a{}_b(X)E_a\). After lowering the first index, metric compatibility gives \(\omega_{ab}=-\omega_{ba}\). Cartan's structure equations are

\[ T^a=de^a+\omega^a{}_b\wedge e^b,\qquad R^a{}_b=d\omega^a{}_b+\omega^a{}_c\wedge\omega^c{}_b. \]

For the Levi-Civita connection, set \(T^a=0\) and solve for \(\omega^a{}_b\). The convention is \(R(X,Y)=\nabla_X\nabla_Y-\nabla_Y\nabla_X-\nabla_{[X,Y]}\). Components follow from \(R^a{}_b=\frac12 R^a{}_{bcd}e^c\wedge e^d\) and \(\mathrm{Ric}_{bd}=R^a{}_{bad}\). With these one can build the Einstein tensor \(\mathrm{Ric}-\tfrac12 R_{\rm scal}g\).

Worked example 23 — Polar frame connection and Christoffel symbols

Take \(e^1=dr\), \(e^2=r\,d\theta\). Then \(de^1=0\) and \(de^2=(1/r)e^1\wedge e^2\). Solving the torsion equations gives \(\omega^2{}_1=d\theta\) and \(\omega^1{}_2=-d\theta\). Consequently \(\nabla_{\partial_\theta}E_1=E_2\) and \(\nabla_{\partial_\theta}E_2=-E_1\), matching the derivatives of the polar unit vectors. Because \(\partial_\theta=rE_2\),

\[ \nabla_{\partial_\theta}\partial_\theta=-r\partial_r,\qquad \nabla_{\partial_r}\partial_\theta=\frac1r\partial_\theta. \]

These are \(\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta}=-r\) and \(\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta}=1/r\). The curvature form is zero since \(d(d\theta)=0\) on each coordinate patch. A rotating frame can have a nonzero connection in flat space.

Worked example 24 — Curvature of a sphere

On a sphere of radius \(a\), take \(e^1=a\,d\vartheta\) and \(e^2=a\sin\vartheta\,d\varphi\). Then \(de^2=(\cot\vartheta/a)e^1\wedge e^2\), so \(\omega^2{}_1=\cos\vartheta\,d\varphi\). The product term in \(R^1{}_2\) vanishes in this two-dimensional orthonormal frame. Thus

\[ R^1{}_2=d(-\cos\vartheta\,d\varphi)=\sin\vartheta\,d\vartheta\wedge d\varphi=\frac1{a^2}e^1\wedge e^2. \]

The Gaussian curvature is \(1/a^2\). Its integral is \(4\pi\). The coframe is local, but the curvature times the area form extends smoothly across the poles.

Worked example 25 — Curvature of a spatially flat expanding universe

For \(ds^2=-dt^2+a(t)^2\sum_i(dx^i)^2\), choose \(e^0=dt\) and \(e^i=a(t)dx^i\). Define \(H=\dot a/a\). Then \(de^i=H e^0\wedge e^i\). Solving the torsion and metric conditions gives \(\omega^i{}_0=\omega^0{}_i=H e^i\) and \(\omega^i{}_j=0\) for spatial indices. The equal signs in the mixed time-space connection follow from the negative time component of the metric. Compute

\[ R^i{}_0=(\dot H+H^2)e^0\wedge e^i,\qquad R^i{}_j=H^2 e^i\wedge e^j. \]

The contraction gives \(\mathrm{Ric}_{00}=-3(\dot H+H^2)\) and \(\mathrm{Ric}_{ij}=(\dot H+3H^2)\delta_{ij}\) in the orthonormal frame. The scalar curvature is \(6(\dot H+2H^2)\), so \(G_{00}=3H^2\). With no cosmological constant, the \(00\) Einstein equation gives \(3H^2=8\pi G\rho\) in units \(c=1\).

10. Gauge theory

A gauge connection is locally a Lie-algebra-valued 1-form \(A\). For matrix-valued forms the wedge combines ordinary exterior multiplication with ordered matrix multiplication. Its curvature is

\[ F_A=dA+A\wedge A. \]

With covariant derivative \(D=d+A\) on vector-valued fields, a gauge change acting on components by \(\psi\mapsto g^{-1}\psi\) sends \(A\mapsto g^{-1}Ag+g^{-1}dg\) and \(F_A\mapsto g^{-1}F_Ag\). The abelian case gives \(F_A=dA\), and for noncommuting matrices the quadratic term can contribute even when the coefficients are constant.

Worked example 26 — Constant noncommuting coefficients

Let \(A=M\,dx+N\,dy\) for constant matrices with \([M,N]\ne0\). Then \(dA=0\), while

\[ F_A=(MN-NM)\,dx\wedge dy. \]

The covariant derivative commutator on a field is \([D_x,D_y]\psi=[M,N]\psi\). Curvature therefore measures the failure of covariant derivatives in different directions to commute.

Worked example 27 — Bianchi identity and a field action

For a matrix-valued 2-form the covariant exterior derivative is \(D_AF_A=dF_A+A\wedge F_A-F_A\wedge A\). Expanding gives \(dF_A=dA\wedge A-A\wedge dA\), which cancels the remaining terms, including the two cubic terms. Thus \(D_AF_A=0\). The same algebra gives the second Bianchi identity for the curvature of the Cartan connection.

For the abelian electromagnetic convention in §5, hold the metric and closed source 3-form \(\mathcal J\) fixed. The action

\[ S[A]=-\frac12\int F\wedge *F+\int A\wedge\mathcal J,\qquad F=dA, \]

has variation \(\delta S=\int \delta A\wedge(\mathcal J-d*F)\) for variations supported away from the boundary. The product rule gives \(d(\delta A\wedge *F)=d\delta A\wedge *F-\delta A\wedge d*F\), so the boundary term drops by Stokes. Stationarity yields \(d*F=\mathcal J\). In the nonabelian case the source-free Yang–Mills action uses an invariant pairing, for example a trace in a matrix representation, in \(\int\operatorname{tr}(F_A\wedge *F_A)\). The variation \(\delta F_A=D_A\delta A\) leads to \(D_A*F_A=0\) under the same boundary assumption.

11. Practice problems with solutions

  1. Exterior derivative. Compute \(d(z\,dx\wedge dy+x\,dy\wedge dz)\).
    Solution. Both terms give \(dx\wedge dy\wedge dz\) after cyclic reordering. The answer is twice that volume form.
  2. Pullback. On the graph \((u,v,uv)\), compute the pullback of \(dz\wedge dx\).
    Solution. Substitute \(dz=v\,du+u\,dv\) and \(dx=du\). The result is \(-u\,du\wedge dv\).
  3. Exactness. Find a primitive for \((2xy+1)dx+x^2dy\) on the plane.
    Solution. Integrating the \(dx\) coefficient gives \(x^2y+x+C(y)\). Matching the \(dy\) coefficient requires \(C'(y)=0\).
  4. Divergence theorem. Find the outward flux of \(X=x\partial_x+y\partial_y+z\partial_z\) through a sphere of radius \(R\).
    Solution. \(d(\iota_Xv_3)=3v_3\). Integrating over the ball gives \(4\pi R^3\). On the sphere \(X\) has normal component \(R\), which gives the same answer.
  5. Maxwell. Let \(A=-\phi(x)dt\) with \(\phi(x)=kx^2/2\). Determine \(\rho\) and \(\mathbf J\) in the conventions of §5.
    Solution. \(e=-kx\,dx\), \(B=0\), and \(\nabla\cdot E=-k\). The field is static, so \(\rho=-k\) and \(\mathbf J=0\).
  6. Hamiltonian mechanics. For \(H=p^2/(2m)+mgq\), obtain the trajectory given \(q(0)=q_0\) and \(p(0)=p_0\).
    Solution. Matching coefficients in \(\iota_X(dq\wedge dp)=dH\) gives \(\dot p=-mg\) and \(\dot q=p/m\). Thus \(p=p_0-mgt\) and \(q=q_0+(p_0/m)t-gt^2/2\).
  7. Thermodynamics. Use \(G=U-TS+PV\) to derive a Maxwell relation in \((T,P)\).
    Solution. \(dG=-S\,dT+V\,dP\). Taking another derivative gives \((\partial S/\partial P)_T=-(\partial V/\partial T)_P\).
  8. Geometry. For \(ds^2=dr^2+f(r)^2d\theta^2\) with \(f>0\), compute Gaussian curvature.
    Solution. Use \(e^1=dr\), \(e^2=f\,d\theta\). The connection is \(\omega^1{}_2=-f'\,d\theta\) and curvature is \(R^1{}_2=-f''\,dr\wedge d\theta=-(f''/f)e^1\wedge e^2\). Taking \(f=r\) gives zero. Taking \(f=a\sin(r/a)\) gives \(1/a^2\).
  9. Fluid mechanics. Find the vorticity of the shear flow \(u=\kappa y\,\partial_x\).
    Solution. \(u^\flat=\kappa y\,dx\), so \(du^\flat=-\kappa dx\wedge dy\) and the three-dimensional curl is \(-\kappa\partial_z\). The circulation around a positively oriented unit square is \(-\kappa\).
  10. Spacetime star. Compute \(**(dt\wedge dx)\) directly.
    Solution. The first star gives \(-dy\wedge dz\). The second gives \(-dt\wedge dx\), consistent with \((-1)^{2(4-2)+1}=-1\).

A calculation checklist

  1. Identify the form degree from what you integrate or measure.
  2. State the metric, orientation, and sign conventions wherever you use a Hodge star or compare physical components.
  3. Distinguish coordinate forms \(dx^i\), whose exterior derivatives vanish, from moving coframe forms \(e^a\).
  4. Pull back to the integration parameters and check the boundary orientation before integrating.
  5. When passing from \(d\omega=0\) to a potential, check the domain and whether the claim is local or global.
