Problem 1: Gauss's (Divergence) Theorem — Flux Across a Closed Cube

Topic: Gauss's (Divergence) Theorem

Statement

Let \(E\) be the solid unit cube bounded by the coordinate planes and the planes \(x = 1\), \(y = 1\), and \(z = 1\), so \(E = \{(x,y,z) \mid 0 \le x \le 1, 0 \le y \le 1, 0 \le z \le 1\}\). Let \(S = \partial E\) be the boundary surface of the cube with outward unit normal vector \(\mathbf{n}\). Consider the vector field: \[\mathbf{F}(x, y, z) = \langle 3x, xy, 2xz \rangle\]

  1. Compute the divergence \(\operatorname{div} \mathbf{F} = \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}\) explicitly.
  2. Apply Gauss's (Divergence) Theorem to evaluate the outward flux \(\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \, dS = \iiint_E \operatorname{div} \mathbf{F} \, dV\).
  3. Verify the result by directly calculating the flux across each of the 6 square faces of the cube.

Solution Steps

  1. Compute Divergence: \[\operatorname{div} \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(3x) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(xy) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(2xz) = 3 + x + 2x = 3 + 3x\].
  2. Apply Divergence Theorem: \[\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \, dS = \int_0^1 \int_0^1 \int_0^1 (3 + 3x) \, dz \, dy \, dx\]. \[\int_0^1 (3 + 3x) \, dx = \left[ 3x + \frac{3}{2}x^2 \right]_0^1 = 3 + \frac{3}{2} = \frac{9}{2}\].
  3. Direct Verification across 6 Faces:
    • Front (\(x=1, \mathbf{n}=\mathbf{i}\)): \(\int_0^1 \int_0^1 F_1(1,y,z) \, dy \, dz = \int_0^1 \int_0^1 3 \, dy \, dz = 3\).
    • Back (\(x=0, \mathbf{n}=-\mathbf{i}\)): \(\int_0^1 \int_0^1 -F_1(0,y,z) \, dy \, dz = 0\).
    • Right (\(y=1, \mathbf{n}=\mathbf{j}\)): \(\int_0^1 \int_0^1 F_2(x,1,z) \, dx \, dz = \int_0^1 \int_0^1 x \, dx \, dz = \frac{1}{2}\).
    • Left (\(y=0, \mathbf{n}=-\mathbf{j}\)): \(\int_0^1 \int_0^1 -F_2(x,0,z) \, dx \, dz = 0\).
    • Top (\(z=1, \mathbf{n}=\mathbf{k}\)): \(\int_0^1 \int_0^1 F_3(x,y,1) \, dx \, dy = \int_0^1 \int_0^1 2x \, dx \, dy = 1\).
    • Bottom (\(z=0, \mathbf{n}=-\mathbf{k}\)): \(\int_0^1 \int_0^1 -F_3(x,y,0) \, dx \, dy = 0\).
    • Total Flux: \(3 + 0 + \frac{1}{2} + 0 + 1 + 0 = \frac{9}{2}\).

Answer

\[\operatorname{div} \mathbf{F} = 3 + 3x; \quad \iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \, dS = \frac{9}{2}\]

Problem 2: Gauss's (Divergence) Theorem — Flux Through a Solid Cylinder

Topic: Gauss's (Divergence) Theorem

Statement

Let \(E\) be the solid cylinder bounded by \(x^2 + y^2 \le 4\), \(z = 0\), and \(z = 3\). Let \(S\) be the complete boundary surface of \(E\) oriented with outward normal \(\mathbf{n}\). Given the vector field: \[\mathbf{F}(x, y, z) = \langle x^3 + y, y^3 - x, z^2 \rangle\]

  1. Compute \(\operatorname{div} \mathbf{F}\) in Cartesian coordinates.
  2. Set up the triple integral for flux over \(E\) in cylindrical coordinates \((r, \theta, z)\).
  3. Evaluate the total outward flux \(\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \, dS\).

Solution Steps

  1. Compute Divergence: \[\operatorname{div} \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^3 + y) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(y^3 - x) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(z^2) = 3x^2 + 3y^2 + 2z\].
  2. Convert to Cylindrical Coordinates: In cylindrical coordinates, \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\) and \(dV = r \, dz \, dr \, d\theta\). The region \(E\) is defined by \(0 \le r \le 2\), \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\), and \(0 \le z \le 3\). \[\operatorname{div} \mathbf{F} = 3r^2 + 2z\]
  3. Evaluate Integral: \[\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \, dS = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^2 \int_0^3 (3r^2 + 2z) r \, dz \, dr \, d\theta\]
    • Inner integral with respect to \(z\): \[\int_0^3 (3r^3 + 2rz) \, dz = \left[ 3r^3 z + r z^2 \right]_0^3 = 9r^3 + 9r\]
    • Middle integral with respect to \(r\): \[\int_0^2 (9r^3 + 9r) \, dr = \left[ \frac{9}{4}r^4 + \frac{9}{2}r^2 \right]_0^2 = \frac{9}{4}(16) + \frac{9}{2}(4) = 36 + 18 = 54\]
    • Outer integral with respect to \(\theta\): \[\int_0^{2\pi} 54 \, d\theta = 108\pi\]

Answer

\[\operatorname{div} \mathbf{F} = 3(x^2 + y^2) + 2z; \quad \iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \, dS = 108\pi\]

Problem 3: Stokes' Theorem — Circulation Around a Paraboloid Boundary

Topic: Stokes' Theorem

Statement

Let \(S\) be the surface of the paraboloid \(z = 4 - x^2 - y^2\) for \(z \ge 0\), oriented with an upward unit normal vector \(\mathbf{n}\). The boundary curve \(C = \partial S\) is the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 4\) in the plane \(z = 0\), traversed counterclockwise when viewed from above. Consider the vector field: \[\mathbf{F}(x, y, z) = \langle -y, x, z^2 \rangle\]

  1. Compute the curl of the vector field \(\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F} = \nabla \times \mathbf{F}\).
  2. Set up and evaluate the surface integral \(\iint_S (\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F}) \cdot \mathbf{n} \, dS\) using Stokes' Theorem.
  3. Verify the result by evaluating the line integral \(\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\) directly via a parameterization of \(C\).

Solution Steps

  1. Compute Curl: \[\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ -y & x & z^2 \end{vmatrix} = \langle 0 - 0, 0 - 0, 1 - (-1) \rangle = \langle 0, 0, 2 \rangle\].
  2. Surface Integral via Stokes' Theorem: For a surface given as \(z = g(x,y) = 4 - x^2 - y^2\), the upward normal vector element is \(d\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{n} \, dS = \langle -g_x, -g_y, 1 \rangle \, dA = \langle 2x, 2y, 1 \rangle \, dA\). \[\iint_S (\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F}) \cdot \mathbf{n} \, dS = \iint_{x^2+y^2 \le 4} \langle 0, 0, 2 \rangle \cdot \langle 2x, 2y, 1 \rangle \, dA = \iint_{x^2+y^2 \le 4} 2 \, dA\]. The area of the disk \(x^2 + y^2 \le 4\) is \(\pi (2^2) = 4\pi\). Therefore, the surface integral equals \(2 \times 4\pi = 8\pi\).
  3. Direct Line Integral Verification: Parameterize \(C\): \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 2\cos t, 2\sin t, 0 \rangle\) for \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\). Then \(d\mathbf{r} = \langle -2\sin t, 2\cos t, 0 \rangle \, dt\). \(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) = \langle -2\sin t, 2\cos t, 0 \rangle\). \[\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_0^{2\pi} \left( (-2\sin t)(-2\sin t) + (2\cos t)(2\cos t) + 0 \right) dt = \int_0^{2\pi} 4 (\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t) \, dt = \int_0^{2\pi} 4 \, dt = 8\pi\].

Answer

\[\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F} = \langle 0, 0, 2 \rangle; \quad \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_S (\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F}) \cdot \mathbf{n} \, dS = 8\pi\]

Problem 4: Stokes' Theorem — Work Done Around a Triangular Path

Topic: Stokes' Theorem

Statement

Let \(C\) be the triangular path in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) connecting the vertices \((1,0,0) \to (0,1,0) \to (0,0,1) \to (1,0,0)\) in order, bounding the planar surface \(S\) given by \(x + y + z = 1\) in the first octant. Orient \(S\) with the upward unit normal \(\mathbf{n}\). Given the force field: \[\mathbf{F}(x, y, z) = \langle x + y^2, y + z^2, z + x^2 \rangle\]

  1. Calculate \(\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F}\).
  2. Parameterize the surface \(S\) and compute the upward normal vector \(\mathbf{N} = \mathbf{n} \, dS\).
  3. Apply Stokes' Theorem to find the work done \(W = \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_S (\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F}) \cdot \mathbf{n} \, dS\).

Solution Steps

  1. Calculate Curl: \[\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ x + y^2 & y + z^2 & z + x^2 \end{vmatrix} = \langle 0 - 2z, 0 - 2x, 0 - 2y \rangle = -2 \langle z, x, y \rangle\].
  2. Parameterize Surface and Normal: The surface is \(z = 1 - x - y\) over the region \(D = \{(x,y) \mid x \ge 0, y \ge 0, x + y \le 1\}\). The upward vector differential is \(d\mathbf{S} = \langle -\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}, -\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}, 1 \rangle \, dA = \langle 1, 1, 1 \rangle \, dA\).
  3. Evaluate Surface Integral: Substitute \(z = 1 - x - y\) into \(\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F}\): \[\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \langle -2(1-x-y), -2x, -2y \rangle \cdot \langle 1, 1, 1 \rangle \, dA = -2(1 - x - y + x + y) \, dA = -2 \, dA\]. \[\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_D (-2) \, dA = -2 \cdot \operatorname{Area}(D)\]. Since \(D\) is a right triangle with base \(1\) and height \(1\), \(\operatorname{Area}(D) = \frac{1}{2}(1)(1) = \frac{1}{2}\). \[W = -2 \left( \frac{1}{2} \right) = -1\]

Answer

\[\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F} = \langle -2z, -2x, -2y \rangle; \quad W = \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = -1\]

Problem 5: Computational Efficiency Comparison — Gauss vs. Direct Method

Topic: Gauss's Theorem / Method Selection for Efficiency

Statement

A student is asked to calculate the outward flux \(\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \, dS\) of the vector field: \[\mathbf{F}(x, y, z) = \langle x^3 + e^{y z}, y^3 + \sin(xz), z^3 + \tan^{-1}(xy) \rangle\] across the closed surface \(S\) of the unit sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\).

  1. Compare Approaches: Compare computing the flux directly via surface parameterization versus using Gauss's (Divergence) Theorem in terms of computational complexity and effort.
  2. Identify the Efficient Method: State which method is preferable and explain why.
  3. Execute Solution: Using the chosen efficient method, show all step-by-step calculations to determine the exact flux.

Solution Steps

  1. Comparison of Methods:
    • Direct Method: Requires parameterizing the spherical surface \(S\), computing normal vectors \(\mathbf{n}\), and evaluating \(\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \, dS\) directly with complicated transcendental components like \(e^{yz}\), \(\sin(xz)\), and \(\tan^{-1}(xy)\) on the sphere.
    • Divergence Theorem: Relates flux to the volume integral \(\iiint_E \operatorname{div} \mathbf{F} \, dV\). Differentiating \(\mathbf{F}\) completely eliminates all complex cross-terms (\(e^{yz}, \sin(xz), \tan^{-1}(xy)\)) because \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(e^{yz}) = 0\), \(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(\sin(xz)) = 0\), and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial z}(\tan^{-1}(xy)) = 0\).
  2. Method Selection: Gauss's (Divergence) Theorem is vastly superior because it reduces a complicated surface integral with transcendental terms to a simple, highly symmetric triple integral over a solid sphere.
  3. Execution via Divergence Theorem:
    • Compute Divergence: \[\operatorname{div} \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^3 + e^{yz}) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(y^3 + \sin(xz)) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(z^3 + \tan^{-1}(xy)) = 3x^2 + 3y^2 + 3z^2\].
    • Set Up in Spherical Coordinates: Using spherical coordinates where \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = \rho^2\) and \(dV = \rho^2 \sin \phi \, d\rho \, d\phi \, d\theta\) over \(0 \le \rho \le 1, 0 \le \phi \le \pi, 0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\). \[\iiint_E 3(x^2 + y^2 + z^2) \, dV = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^\pi \int_0^1 3\rho^2 \cdot \rho^2 \sin \phi \, d\rho \, d\phi \, d\theta\].
    • Evaluate Integral: \[\int_0^1 3\rho^4 \, d\rho = \left[ \frac{3}{5}\rho^5 \right]_0^1 = \frac{3}{5}\] \[\int_0^\pi \sin \phi \, d\phi = 2\] \[\int_0^{2\pi} d\theta = 2\pi\] \[\text{Total Flux} = \frac{3}{5} \times 2 \times 2\pi = \frac{12\pi}{5}\].

Answer

Gauss's Theorem is chosen because \(\operatorname{div} \mathbf{F} = 3(x^2 + y^2 + z^2)\) eliminates transcendental terms. The exact total flux is: \[\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \, dS = \frac{12\pi}{5}\]

Problem 6: Gauss's Theorem — Position Field Through a Sphere

Topic: Divergence Theorem

Statement

Let \(\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = x\,\mathbf{i} + y\,\mathbf{j} + z\,\mathbf{k}\) and let \(S\) be the sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2=a^2\) with outward orientation. Compute the outward flux \(\displaystyle\oiint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS\).

Then check your answer by evaluating the surface integral directly.

Hint: On the sphere, \(\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{r}/a\) where \(\mathbf{r}=(x,y,z)\).

Solution Steps

  1. Via the Divergence Theorem: The divergence is constant: \[\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial x}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial y}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial z}{\partial z} = 3.\] Therefore \[\oiint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \iiint_V 3\,dV = 3\cdot\operatorname{Vol}(V) = 3\cdot\tfrac{4}{3}\pi a^3 = 4\pi a^3.\]
  2. Direct Check: On the sphere \(\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{r}/|\mathbf{r}| = \mathbf{r}/a\), and \(\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{r}\), so \[\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n} = \frac{\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{r}}{a} = \frac{a^2}{a} = a,\] a constant on \(S\). Hence the flux is \(a\cdot\operatorname{Area}(S) = a\cdot 4\pi a^2 = 4\pi a^3\).

Remark: Because \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{r}=3\), the identity \(\oiint_S \mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = 3\operatorname{Vol}(V)\) holds for every closed surface.

Answer

\[4\pi a^3\]

Problem 7: Gauss's Theorem — Flux Through the Unit Cube

Topic: Divergence Theorem

Statement

Let \(\mathbf{F} = x^2\,\mathbf{i} + y^2\,\mathbf{j} + z^2\,\mathbf{k}\) and let \(S\) be the boundary of the cube \(V=[0,1]\times[0,1]\times[0,1]\), outward oriented. Find the total outward flux.

How much work would the direct approach have been?

Solution Steps

  1. Compute Divergence: \[\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = 2x + 2y + 2z.\] By the divergence theorem, \[\oiint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1 (2x+2y+2z)\,dx\,dy\,dz.\] By symmetry the three terms are equal, and \[\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1 2x\,dx\,dy\,dz = 2\cdot\tfrac12 = 1.\] So the total is \(1+1+1 = 3\).
  2. Direct Verification: Six faces. On the face \(x=1\) (normal \(\mathbf{i}\)) the integrand is \(x^2=1\), giving \(+1\); on \(x=0\) (normal \(-\mathbf{i}\)) the integrand is \(-x^2=0\), giving \(0\). Same for the \(y\) and \(z\) pairs: \(1+0+1+0+1+0 = 3\).

Here the direct computation is easy, but it required six separate parametrizations and six sign checks. That bookkeeping is exactly what the divergence theorem removes.

Answer

\[3\]

Problem 8: Stokes' Theorem — Verifying Stokes on a Disk

Topic: Stokes' Theorem

Statement

Let \(\mathbf{F} = -y\,\mathbf{i} + x\,\mathbf{j}\) and let \(C\) be the unit circle \(x^2+y^2=1\) in the plane \(z=0\), traversed counterclockwise when viewed from the positive \(z\)-axis.

  1. Compute \(\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) directly.
  2. Compute \(\iint_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS\) where \(S\) is the unit disk with upward normal, and confirm the two agree.

Solution Steps

  1. Line Integral: Parametrize \(\mathbf{r}(t) = (\cos t,\ \sin t,\ 0)\), \(t\in[0,2\pi]\), so \(d\mathbf{r} = (-\sin t,\ \cos t,\ 0)\,dt\). Along \(C\), \[\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) = (-\sin t,\ \cos t,\ 0),\] \[\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = (\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t)\,dt = dt.\] Hence \(\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_0^{2\pi} dt = 2\pi\).
  2. Curl Integral: \[\nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \begin{vmatrix}\mathbf{i}&\mathbf{j}&\mathbf{k}\\[2pt] \partial_x&\partial_y&\partial_z\\[2pt] -y&x&0\end{vmatrix} = (0,\,0,\,2).\] With \(\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{k}\) on the disk, \[\iint_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \iint_D 2\,dA = 2\cdot\pi(1)^2 = 2\pi.\]

Interpretation: \(\mathbf{F}\) is the velocity field of rigid rotation with angular velocity \(\boldsymbol{\omega} = \mathbf{k}\), and indeed \(\nabla\times\mathbf{F} = 2\boldsymbol{\omega}\). Curl measures local spin; Stokes says the total local spin over the disk equals the net circulation around its rim.

Answer

Both equal \(2\pi\)

Problem 9: Gauss's Theorem — Cubic Field Through a Solid Cylinder

Topic: Divergence Theorem / Cylindrical Coordinates

Statement

Let \(\mathbf{F} = x^3\,\mathbf{i} + y^3\,\mathbf{j} + z^3\,\mathbf{k}\) and let \(S\) be the closed boundary of the solid cylinder \[V = \{(x,y,z) : x^2+y^2 \le a^2,\ 0\le z\le h\}\] (lateral wall, top disk, bottom disk), outward oriented. Find the total outward flux.

Hint: In cylindrical coordinates \(x^2+y^2 = r^2\) and \(dV = r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz\).

Solution Steps

  1. Compute Divergence: \[\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = 3x^2 + 3y^2 + 3z^2 = 3\big[(x^2+y^2) + z^2\big].\] So \[\oiint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = 3\iiint_V (x^2+y^2)\,dV + 3\iiint_V z^2\,dV.\]
  2. First Piece: In cylindrical coordinates \(x^2+y^2 = r^2\): \[\iiint_V (x^2+y^2)\,dV = \int_0^h\!\!\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^a r^2\cdot r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz = h\cdot 2\pi\cdot\frac{a^4}{4} = \frac{\pi a^4 h}{2}.\]
  3. Second Piece: The cross-sectional area is \(\pi a^2\) for every \(z\), so \[\iiint_V z^2\,dV = \pi a^2\int_0^h z^2\,dz = \frac{\pi a^2 h^3}{3}.\]
  4. Combine: \[\oiint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = 3\left(\frac{\pi a^4 h}{2}\right) + 3\left(\frac{\pi a^2 h^3}{3}\right) = \frac{3\pi a^4 h}{2} + \pi a^2 h^3.\]

Sanity check on dimensions: both terms scale like (length)\(^5\), consistent with \(\mathbf{F}\sim L^3\) integrated over an area \(\sim L^2\).

Answer

\[\frac{3\pi a^4 h}{2} + \pi a^2 h^3 = \pi a^2 h\left(\frac{3a^2}{2} + h^2\right)\]

Problem 10: Stokes' Theorem — Circulation on a Tilted Circle

Topic: Stokes' Theorem / Projecting onto a Plane

Statement

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) where \[\mathbf{F} = -y^2\,\mathbf{i} + x\,\mathbf{j} + z^2\,\mathbf{k}\] and \(C\) is the curve of intersection of the plane \(y+z=2\) with the cylinder \(x^2+y^2=1\), oriented counterclockwise as seen from above.

Hint: The curve bounds the elliptical piece of the plane \(z=2-y\) lying over the unit disk. For a graph \(z=g(x,y)\) with upward orientation, \(\mathbf{n}\,dS = (-g_x,\,-g_y,\,1)\,dA\).

Solution Steps

  1. Compute Curl: With \(P=-y^2\), \(Q=x\), \(R=z^2\): \[\nabla\times\mathbf{F} = (R_y - Q_z,\ P_z - R_x,\ Q_x - P_y) = (0-0,\ 0-0,\ 1-(-2y)) = (0,\ 0,\ 1+2y).\]
  2. Choose the Surface: Take \(S\) to be the flat piece of the plane \(z = g(x,y) = 2-y\) lying above the unit disk \(D=\{x^2+y^2\le 1\}\), with upward normal. Then \[\mathbf{n}\,dS = (-g_x,\,-g_y,\,1)\,dA = (0,\ 1,\ 1)\,dA.\]
  3. Integrate: \[(\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot(0,1,1) = (0)(0) + (0)(1) + (1+2y)(1) = 1+2y,\] so \[\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_D (1+2y)\,dA.\] The term \(\iint_D 2y\,dA\) vanishes because \(D\) is symmetric about the \(x\)-axis and \(y\) is odd in \(y\). What remains is \(\iint_D 1\,dA = \operatorname{Area}(D) = \pi\).

Notice the two \(z\)-dependent pieces of \(\mathbf{F}\) never entered: \(z^2\mathbf{k}\) is a gradient field, so it contributes nothing to any closed circulation.

Answer

\[\pi\]

Problem 11: Gauss's Theorem — An Open Hemisphere: Close It Off

Topic: Divergence Theorem / Adding a Cap

Statement

Let \(\mathbf{F} = x\,\mathbf{i} + y\,\mathbf{j} + (z+1)\,\mathbf{k}\) and let \(H\) be the open upper hemisphere \[H = \{x^2+y^2+z^2 = a^2,\ z\ge 0\},\] oriented with the normal pointing away from the origin. Compute \(\displaystyle\iint_H \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS\).

The divergence theorem needs a closed surface — so build one.

Solution Steps

  1. Close the Surface: Let \(D\) be the flat disk \(\{x^2+y^2\le a^2,\ z=0\}\). Then \(S = H\cup D\) is the closed boundary of the solid half-ball \(V\). For \(S\) to carry the outward orientation, \(D\) must take the downward normal \(\mathbf{n}_D = -\mathbf{k}\). Thus \[\underbrace{\oiint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS}_{\text{divergence theorem}} = \iint_H \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS + \iint_D \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}_D\,dS.\]
  2. The Closed Flux: \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = 1+1+1 = 3\), and \(V\) is a half-ball of volume \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac43\pi a^3 = \tfrac23\pi a^3\). So \[\oiint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = 3\cdot\frac{2}{3}\pi a^3 = 2\pi a^3.\]
  3. The Cap: On \(D\) we have \(z=0\), so \(\mathbf{F} = (x,\,y,\,1)\) and \[\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}_D = \mathbf{F}\cdot(0,0,-1) = -1.\] Therefore \(\displaystyle\iint_D \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}_D\,dS = -\operatorname{Area}(D) = -\pi a^2\).
  4. Subtract: \[\iint_H \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = 2\pi a^3 - (-\pi a^2) = 2\pi a^3 + \pi a^2.\]

Common trap: Forgetting that the cap's normal must point down. Getting the sign wrong here would give \(2\pi a^3 - \pi a^2\).

Answer

\[2\pi a^3 + \pi a^2\]

Problem 12: Stokes' Theorem — Surface Independence: Paraboloid vs. Disk

Topic: Stokes' Theorem / Two Surfaces, One Boundary

Statement

Let \(\mathbf{F} = y\,\mathbf{i} + z\,\mathbf{j} + x\,\mathbf{k}\) and let \(S\) be the part of the paraboloid \(z = 1 - x^2 - y^2\) with \(z\ge 0\), oriented upward.

  1. Compute \(\displaystyle\iint_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS\) directly.
  2. Recompute it by replacing \(S\) with the flat unit disk in \(z=0\), and explain why this is legitimate.

Solution Steps

  1. Compute Curl: With \(P=y\), \(Q=z\), \(R=x\): \[\nabla\times\mathbf{F} = (R_y-Q_z,\ P_z-R_x,\ Q_x-P_y) = (0-1,\ 0-1,\ 0-1) = (-1,\,-1,\,-1).\]
  2. Over the Paraboloid: Writing \(z = g(x,y) = 1-x^2-y^2\) with upward orientation, \[\mathbf{n}\,dS = (-g_x,\,-g_y,\,1)\,dA = (2x,\ 2y,\ 1)\,dA,\] over the shadow region \(D = \{x^2+y^2\le 1\}\). Then \[(\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot(2x,2y,1) = -2x - 2y - 1,\] \[\iint_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \iint_D (-2x-2y-1)\,dA.\] The \(x\) and \(y\) terms integrate to zero by symmetry of the disk, leaving \(-\operatorname{Area}(D) = -\pi\).
  3. Over the Flat Disk: Both surfaces have the same boundary curve \(C:\ x^2+y^2=1,\ z=0\), and the upward normals induce the same orientation on \(C\). By Stokes both flux integrals equal \(\oint_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\), hence they are equal. On the flat disk \(\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{k}\), so \[\iint_{D} (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{k}\,dA = \iint_D (-1)\,dA = -\pi.\]
  4. Why It's Legitimate: If \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) are two oriented surfaces sharing the same boundary curve with the same induced orientation, and \(\mathbf{F}\) is \(C^1\) on a region containing both, then \[\iint_{S_1}(\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_{S_2}(\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS.\] Equivalently: \(S_1\) glued to \(-S_2\) is a closed surface, and by the divergence theorem the flux of a curl through any closed surface is zero, since \(\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\equiv 0\).

Answer

\(-\pi\) by either route

Problem 13: Gauss's Theorem — Volume as a Surface Integral (Ellipsoid)

Topic: Divergence Theorem / Turning a Theorem into a Tool

Statement

  1. Show that for any solid region \(V\) with piecewise-smooth boundary \(S\), \[\operatorname{Vol}(V) = \frac{1}{3}\oiint_{S}\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS, \qquad \mathbf{r}=(x,y,z).\]
  2. Use this to compute the volume enclosed by the ellipsoid \(\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}+\dfrac{z^2}{c^2}=1\), using the parametrization \[\mathbf{r}(\varphi,\theta) = (a\sin\varphi\cos\theta,\ b\sin\varphi\sin\theta,\ c\cos\varphi), \quad \varphi\in[0,\pi],\ \theta\in[0,2\pi].\]

Solution Steps

  1. Prove the Identity: Since \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{r} = 3\), the divergence theorem gives \[\oiint_S \mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \iiint_V 3\,dV = 3\operatorname{Vol}(V),\] and dividing by 3 gives the identity. (This is the 3-D analogue of the shoelace/Green's-theorem area formula \(A = \tfrac12\oint (x\,dy - y\,dx)\).)
  2. Compute the Cross Product: We need \(\mathbf{r}\cdot(\mathbf{r}_\varphi\times\mathbf{r}_\theta)\). Differentiating the parametrization, \[\mathbf{r}_\varphi = (a\cos\varphi\cos\theta,\ b\cos\varphi\sin\theta,\ -c\sin\varphi),\] \[\mathbf{r}_\theta = (-a\sin\varphi\sin\theta,\ b\sin\varphi\cos\theta,\ 0).\] Taking the cross product: \[\mathbf{r}_\varphi\times\mathbf{r}_\theta = (bc\sin^2\varphi\cos\theta,\ ac\sin^2\varphi\sin\theta,\ ab\sin\varphi\cos\varphi).\]
  3. Dot with \(\mathbf{r}\): \[\mathbf{r}\cdot(\mathbf{r}_\varphi\times\mathbf{r}_\theta) = abc\sin^3\varphi\cos^2\theta + abc\sin^3\varphi\sin^2\theta + abc\sin\varphi\cos^2\varphi\] \[= abc\sin^3\varphi + abc\sin\varphi\cos^2\varphi = abc\sin\varphi.\]
  4. Integrate: \[\oiint_S \mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi} abc\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\theta = abc\cdot 2\pi\cdot 2 = 4\pi abc,\] and \[\operatorname{Vol} = \frac{1}{3}(4\pi abc) = \frac{4}{3}\pi abc.\] Setting \(a=b=c=R\) recovers \(\tfrac43\pi R^3\).

Answer

\[\operatorname{Vol} = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi abc\]

Problem 14: Gauss's Theorem — The Inverse-Square Field and Gauss's Law

Topic: Divergence Theorem / Excising a Singularity

Statement

Let \[\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{\mathbf{r}}{|\mathbf{r}|^3} = \frac{\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{r^2}, \qquad \mathbf{r}=(x,y,z)\ne\mathbf{0}.\]

  1. Show that \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = 0\) everywhere except at the origin.
  2. Let \(S\) be a closed surface that does not enclose the origin. Show the outward flux is \(0\).
  3. Let \(S\) be any closed surface that does enclose the origin. Show the outward flux is \(4\pi\) — regardless of the shape of \(S\).

Hint for (3): The divergence theorem does not apply directly, because \(\mathbf{F}\) is not \(C^1\) on the enclosed solid. Remove a small ball around the origin and apply the theorem to what's left.

Solution Steps

  1. The Divergence Vanishes: Use the product rule \(\nabla\cdot(f\mathbf{G}) = f\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{G} + \nabla f\cdot\mathbf{G}\) with \(f = r^{-3}\) and \(\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{r}\). Since \(\nabla r = \hat{\mathbf{r}}\) we get \(\nabla(r^{-3}) = -3r^{-4}\hat{\mathbf{r}}\), and so \[\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{\mathbf{r}}{r^3}\right) = \frac{1}{r^3}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{r}) + \left(-\frac{3}{r^4}\hat{\mathbf{r}}\right)\cdot\mathbf{r} = \frac{3}{r^3} - \frac{3}{r^4}\,r = \frac{3}{r^3} - \frac{3}{r^3} = 0,\] valid for all \(r>0\).
  2. Origin Outside: If \(S=\partial V\) and \(\mathbf{0}\notin V\), then \(\mathbf{F}\) is \(C^1\) on an open set containing \(V\), so the divergence theorem applies: \[\oiint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \iiint_V 0\,dV = 0.\]
  3. Origin Inside: Choose \(\varepsilon>0\) small enough that the ball \(B_\varepsilon = \{r\le\varepsilon\}\) lies entirely inside \(S\), and let \(W = V \setminus B_\varepsilon\) be the shell between them. \(\mathbf{F}\) is \(C^1\) on \(W\), whose boundary consists of \(S\) (outward) together with the sphere \(S_\varepsilon\) oriented inward toward the origin. So \[0 = \iiint_W \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\,dV = \oiint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS - \oiint_{S_\varepsilon} \mathbf{F}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}\,dS,\] hence \[\oiint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \oiint_{S_\varepsilon} \mathbf{F}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}\,dS.\] On \(S_\varepsilon\) the integrand is \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}} = 1/\varepsilon^2\), a constant, and \(\operatorname{Area}(S_\varepsilon) = 4\pi\varepsilon^2\). Therefore \[\oiint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \frac{1}{\varepsilon^2}\cdot 4\pi\varepsilon^2 = 4\pi,\] independent of \(\varepsilon\) and of the shape of \(S\).

Physical significance: The electric field of a point charge \(q\) at the origin is \(\mathbf{E} = \dfrac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{r^2}\), so part (3) gives \(\oiint_S \mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = q/\epsilon_0\), which is Gauss's law in integral form. In distributional language, \(\nabla\cdot(\hat{\mathbf{r}}/r^2) = 4\pi\,\delta^3(\mathbf{r})\).

Answer

\(0\) if the origin is outside; \(4\pi\) if it is inside

Problem 15: Stokes' Theorem — A Curl-Free Field with Nonzero Circulation

Topic: Stokes' Theorem / Where the Hypotheses Bite

Statement

Consider the "vortex" field, defined everywhere except on the \(z\)-axis: \[\mathbf{F} = \frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\,\mathbf{i} + \frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\,\mathbf{j}.\]

  1. Show that \(\nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\) on its whole domain.
  2. Compute \(\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) for \(C\) the unit circle in the plane \(z=0\), counterclockwise.
  3. These two results appear to contradict Stokes' theorem. Explain precisely which hypothesis fails.
  4. What is \(\oint_{C'}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) for a closed curve \(C'\) in the plane \(z=0\) that does not wind around the origin?

Solution Steps

  1. The Curl Is Zero: Write \(P = -y/(x^2+y^2)\), \(Q = x/(x^2+y^2)\), \(R=0\). Since \(P,Q\) do not depend on \(z\) and \(R\equiv 0\), the \(\mathbf{i}\)- and \(\mathbf{j}\)-components of the curl vanish. For the \(\mathbf{k}\)-component: \[\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = \frac{(x^2+y^2)\cdot 1 - x\cdot 2x}{(x^2+y^2)^2} = \frac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2},\] \[\frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \frac{-(x^2+y^2) - (-y)(2y)}{(x^2+y^2)^2} = \frac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}.\] They are identical, so \(Q_x - P_y = 0\) and \(\nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\) everywhere on \(\mathbb{R}^3\setminus\{x=y=0\}\).
  2. The Circulation Is Not Zero: With \(\mathbf{r}(t) = (\cos t,\sin t,0)\) we have \(x^2+y^2=1\), so \(\mathbf{F} = (-\sin t,\ \cos t,\ 0)\) and \(d\mathbf{r} = (-\sin t,\ \cos t,\ 0)\,dt\). Then \[\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = (\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t)\,dt = dt \implies \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 2\pi.\]
  3. Which Hypothesis Fails: Stokes' theorem requires a surface \(S\) with \(\partial S = C\) on which \(\mathbf{F}\) is \(C^1\). But \(C\) encircles the \(z\)-axis, and every surface whose boundary is \(C\) must intersect the \(z\)-axis at least once. On that intersection point \(\mathbf{F}\) is undefined, so no admissible \(S\) exists and Stokes simply does not apply. The structural reason is that the domain \(\mathbb{R}^3\setminus z\text{-axis}\) is not simply connected. Locally, \(\mathbf{F}=\nabla\theta\), where \(\theta\) is a continuous branch of \(\arg(x+iy)\). There is no single-valued continuous angle on the entire punctured plane: following a continuous angle once around \(C\) increases it by \(2\pi\).
  4. Curves That Miss the Origin: If \(C'\) is a simple closed curve whose interior avoids the origin, it bounds a planar region \(D'\) on whose neighbourhood \(\mathbf{F}\) is \(C^1\). Stokes applies and gives \[\oint_{C'}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_{D'}(\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{k}\,dA = 0.\] For any piecewise-smooth closed curve avoiding the axis, including self-intersecting curves, follow a continuous angle along its planar projection. Then \(\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}=d\theta\), and the net angle change is \(2\pi n\), where \(n\) is its winding number about the axis. Thus \(\oint_{C'}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}=2\pi n\), which is zero whenever the winding number is zero, without requiring that \(C'\) bound a single planar region avoiding the origin.

Physical connection: The magnetic field of a straight current-carrying wire is exactly this field (up to constants), and Ampere's law \(\oint\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}\) is the physical statement of "circulation counts what you enclose". The same phenomenon is the residue theorem in complex analysis: \(\oint dz/z = 2\pi i\).

Answer

(1) \(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0}\) · (2) \(2\pi\) · (3) No admissible surface exists; the domain is not simply connected · (4) \(0\)
