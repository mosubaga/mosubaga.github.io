Problem 1 — One-Sample Mean: Battery Life

A manufacturer advertises that a new rechargeable battery has a mean operating life of \(50\) hours. A quality-control engineer randomly selects \(n=25\) batteries and obtains

\[ \bar{x}=52.4 \text{ hours}, \qquad s=6.0 \text{ hours}. \]

Assume that battery lifetimes are approximately normally distributed and that the sampled batteries are independent.

Use \(\alpha=0.05\).

Questions

  1. State the null and alternative hypotheses.
  2. Identify the appropriate test statistic.
  3. Perform the hypothesis test.
  4. Construct a \(95\%\) confidence interval for the true mean battery life.
  5. Explain the relationship between the hypothesis-test conclusion and the confidence interval.
  6. Suppose the manufacturer considers an improvement of at least \(5\) hours to be practically important. Discuss statistical versus practical significance.

Solution

Let

\[ \mu=\text{true mean operating life of the new battery}. \]

Because the population standard deviation is unknown and the sample is relatively small, a one-sample \(t\)-procedure is appropriate.

Step 1: Hypotheses

\[ H_0:\mu=50 \]

\[ H_a:\mu\neq 50. \]

This is a two-tailed test.

Step 2: Test statistic

The test statistic is

\[ t=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}}. \]

Substituting the observed values,

\[ t=\frac{52.4-50}{6/\sqrt{25}} =\frac{2.4}{1.2} =2.00. \]

The degrees of freedom are

\[ df=n-1=24. \]

For a two-tailed test with \(\alpha=0.05\),

\[ t_{0.025,24}\approx 2.064. \]

Thus, the rejection rule is

\[ |t|>2.064. \]

Because

\[ |2.00|<2.064, \]

we do not reject \(H_0\).

The two-sided p-value is approximately

\[ p\approx 0.0569. \]

Since

\[ p=0.0569>0.05, \]

the conclusion is again to fail to reject \(H_0\).

\[ \boxed{\text{There is insufficient evidence at the }5\%\text{ level that }\mu\neq 50.} \]

Step 3: \(95\%\) confidence interval

The confidence interval is

\[ \bar{x}\pm t_{\alpha/2,df}\frac{s}{\sqrt n}. \]

Therefore,

\[ 52.4\pm 2.064\left(\frac{6}{5}\right). \]

The margin of error is

\[ 2.064(1.2)=2.4768. \]

Hence,

\[ (52.4-2.4768,\;52.4+2.4768) \]

or approximately

\[ \boxed{(49.92,\;54.88)\text{ hours}}. \]

Because \(50\) hours lies inside the \(95\%\) confidence interval, the interval is consistent with the failure to reject

\[ H_0:\mu=50. \]

Interpretation

The sample suggests an average increase of

\[ 52.4-50=2.4\text{ hours}, \]

but the evidence is not quite strong enough to establish a statistically significant difference at \(\alpha=0.05\).

The company considers a \(5\)-hour improvement practically important. The observed improvement of \(2.4\) hours is below this benchmark, so even if a similar effect were eventually shown to be statistically significant with a larger sample, it might still not be practically important.

Assumptions

The analysis assumes:

\[ \boxed{\text{independent observations}} \]

and an approximately normal population distribution because \(n=25\) is modest.

If the data were strongly skewed or contained major outliers, the one-sample \(t\)-procedure could be questionable.

Common Pitfalls

  • Using a \(z\)-test even though the population standard deviation \(\sigma\) is unknown.
  • Concluding that \(H_0\) is proven true because it was not rejected.
  • Saying that \(p=0.0569\) is the probability that \(H_0\) is true.
  • Ignoring practical significance simply because the sample mean exceeds \(50\).

Problem 2 — One-Sample Proportion: Appointment Reminders

A public health clinic historically finds that \(60\%\) of patients attend their scheduled follow-up appointments. After introducing a text-message reminder system, the clinic randomly observes \(120\) patients, of whom \(84\) attend.

Assume patients can reasonably be treated as independent and that the sample represents patients receiving the reminder system.

Use \(\alpha=0.05\).

Questions

  1. Test whether the attendance proportion differs from the historical value of \(0.60\).
  2. Construct a \(95\%\) confidence interval for the true attendance proportion.
  3. Interpret the results in context.
  4. The clinic considers an improvement of at least \(5\) percentage points to be practically meaningful. Discuss practical significance.

Solution

Let

\[ p=\text{true attendance proportion under the reminder system}. \]

The observed sample proportion is

\[ \hat p=\frac{84}{120}=0.70. \]

Step 1: Hypotheses

\[ H_0:p=0.60 \]

\[ H_a:p\neq 0.60. \]

Step 2: Check conditions

Under \(H_0\),

\[ np_0=120(0.60)=72 \]

and

\[ n(1-p_0)=120(0.40)=48. \]

Both are greater than \(10\), so the normal approximation is appropriate.

Step 3: Test statistic

For a one-sample proportion test,

\[ z= \frac{\hat p-p_0} {\sqrt{\frac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n}}}. \]

Thus,

\[ z= \frac{0.70-0.60} {\sqrt{\frac{0.60(0.40)}{120}}}. \]

The null standard error is

\[ \sqrt{\frac{0.24}{120}} = \sqrt{0.002} \approx 0.04472. \]

Therefore,

\[ z=\frac{0.10}{0.04472} \approx 2.236. \]

For a two-tailed test at \(\alpha=0.05\), the critical values are

\[ z=\pm1.96. \]

Since

\[ 2.236>1.96, \]

we reject \(H_0\).

The two-sided p-value is approximately

\[ p\approx0.0253. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{\text{There is statistically significant evidence that the attendance rate differs from }60\%.} \]

Because the observed rate is \(70\%\), the sample suggests that attendance increased.

Step 4: \(95\%\) confidence interval

For estimation, use the estimated standard error

\[ SE= \sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}}. \]

Therefore,

\[ SE= \sqrt{\frac{0.70(0.30)}{120}} = \sqrt{0.00175} \approx0.04183. \]

The interval is

\[ 0.70\pm1.96(0.04183). \]

The margin of error is approximately

\[ 0.08199. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{(0.618,\;0.782)}. \]

Equivalently, the clinic can estimate the true attendance percentage to be between approximately

\[ 61.8\% \text{ and }78.2\%. \]

The null value \(0.60\) is not in this confidence interval, which agrees with the rejection of \(H_0\).

Statistical versus practical significance

The observed improvement is

\[ 0.70-0.60=0.10, \]

or \(10\) percentage points.

This exceeds the clinic's practical benchmark of \(5\) percentage points.

However, the confidence interval for the attendance proportion begins at approximately \(61.8\%\), corresponding to an improvement of only about

\[ 61.8\%-60\%=1.8 \]

percentage points.

Thus, although the data establish a statistically detectable improvement, they do not establish with \(95\%\) confidence that the improvement is at least \(5\) percentage points.

Common Pitfalls

  • Using \(\hat p\) instead of \(p_0\) in the denominator of the hypothesis-test statistic.
  • Forgetting that the test is two-sided.
  • Interpreting a \(95\%\) confidence interval as saying there is a \(95\%\) probability that \(p\) lies in this particular computed interval.
  • Equating statistical significance with guaranteed practical importance.

Problem 3 — Independent Two-Sample Means: Rehabilitation Programs

A hospital compares two independent physical-rehabilitation programs. Improvement is measured using a mobility score, where larger positive values indicate greater improvement.

The results are:

\[ \begin{array}{c|ccc} & n & \bar{x} & s\\ \hline \text{Program A} & 40 & 18.2 & 4.5\\ \text{Program B} & 35 & 15.6 & 5.1 \end{array} \]

Assume that patients were independently assigned to the two programs, observations are independent within groups, and the score distributions contain no severe outliers. Do not assume equal population variances.

Use \(\alpha=0.05\).

Questions

  1. Test whether the two programs have different mean improvements.
  2. Construct a \(95\%\) confidence interval for \[ \mu_A-\mu_B. \]
  3. Interpret the result.
  4. Explain why Welch's two-sample \(t\)-test is preferred here over a pooled \(t\)-test.

Solution

Define

\[ \mu_A=\text{mean improvement for Program A} \]

and

\[ \mu_B=\text{mean improvement for Program B}. \]

Step 1: Hypotheses

\[ H_0:\mu_A-\mu_B=0 \]

\[ H_a:\mu_A-\mu_B\neq0. \]

Step 2: Difference in sample means

\[ \bar{x}_A-\bar{x}_B = 18.2-15.6 = 2.6. \]

Step 3: Standard error

Using Welch's procedure,

\[ SE= \sqrt{\frac{s_A^2}{n_A}+\frac{s_B^2}{n_B}}. \]

Therefore,

\[ SE= \sqrt{\frac{4.5^2}{40}+\frac{5.1^2}{35}}. \]

Calculate each component:

\[ \frac{4.5^2}{40} = \frac{20.25}{40} = 0.50625 \]

and

\[ \frac{5.1^2}{35} = \frac{26.01}{35} \approx0.74314. \]

Thus,

\[ SE= \sqrt{0.50625+0.74314} = \sqrt{1.24939} \approx1.1178. \]

Step 4: Test statistic

\[ t= \frac{2.6-0}{1.1178} \approx2.326. \]

Using the Welch-Satterthwaite approximation,

\[ df\approx68.42. \]

The approximate two-sided critical value is

\[ t_{0.025,68.42}\approx1.995. \]

Since

\[ |2.326|>1.995, \]

we reject \(H_0\).

The p-value is approximately

\[ p\approx0.0230. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\text{There is statistically significant evidence that the two mean improvements differ.}} \]

Step 5: Confidence interval

The \(95\%\) confidence interval is

\[ (\bar{x}_A-\bar{x}_B) \pm t^*SE. \]

Thus,

\[ 2.6\pm1.995(1.1178). \]

The margin of error is approximately

\[ 2.230. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{(0.37,\;4.83)}. \]

Interpretation

We estimate that Program A improves the mobility score by between approximately

\[ 0.37 \]

and

\[ 4.83 \]

points more than Program B on average.

Because \(0\) is not in the interval, this conclusion is consistent with rejection of the null hypothesis.

Whether the difference is practically important requires clinical context. For example, if clinicians consider a \(4\)-point difference necessary to matter to patients, the observed mean difference of \(2.6\) points may have limited practical importance despite its statistical significance.

Why use Welch's test?

The pooled two-sample test assumes

\[ \sigma_A^2=\sigma_B^2. \]

That assumption was not given.

Welch's test does not require equal population variances and is therefore the safer choice.

Assumptions

The procedure assumes:

\[ \boxed{\text{independence between the two groups}} \]

and independence of observations within each group.

The sample sizes are moderately large, so the \(t\)-procedure is relatively robust to modest nonnormality, although severe skewness or extreme outliers should still be investigated.

Common Pitfalls

  • Treating the two independent groups as paired data.
  • Automatically using a pooled test without checking the equal-variance assumption.
  • Reversing the subtraction and then misinterpreting the sign.
  • Saying that statistical significance proves Program A is meaningfully better in practice.

Problem 4 — Paired Samples: Sleep Before and After an Intervention

A researcher studies whether a behavioral program changes the amount of time university students spend using their phones after 10 p.m.

For \(16\) students, phone use is recorded before and after the intervention. Define each paired difference as

\[ d=\text{after}-\text{before}. \]

The sample of \(16\) differences has

\[ \bar d=-3.2\text{ hours per week} \]

and

\[ s_d=4.0\text{ hours}. \]

Assume the students are independent of one another and that the distribution of paired differences is approximately normal.

Use \(\alpha=0.05\).

Questions

  1. Test whether the intervention changes average late-night phone use.
  2. Construct a \(95\%\) confidence interval for the mean paired difference.
  3. Interpret the negative sign.
  4. Explain why an independent two-sample test would be inappropriate.

Solution

Let

\[ \mu_d=\text{population mean of the paired differences}. \]

Step 1: Hypotheses

\[ H_0:\mu_d=0 \]

\[ H_a:\mu_d\neq0. \]

Step 2: Standard error

Because

\[ n=16, \]

the standard error is

\[ SE=\frac{s_d}{\sqrt n} = \frac{4.0}{\sqrt{16}} = 1.0. \]

Step 3: Test statistic

\[ t= \frac{\bar d-0}{s_d/\sqrt n} = \frac{-3.2}{1.0} = -3.2. \]

The degrees of freedom are

\[ df=16-1=15. \]

For a two-tailed test with \(\alpha=0.05\),

\[ t_{0.025,15}\approx2.131. \]

Since

\[ |-3.2|>2.131, \]

we reject \(H_0\).

The two-sided p-value is approximately

\[ p\approx0.0060. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{\text{There is strong evidence that the intervention changes average late-night phone use.}} \]

Step 4: Confidence interval

The interval is

\[ \bar d\pm t^*\frac{s_d}{\sqrt n}. \]

Therefore,

\[ -3.2\pm2.131(1.0). \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{(-5.33,\;-1.07)\text{ hours per week}}. \]

Interpretation

Because the differences were defined as

\[ d=\text{after}-\text{before}, \]

a negative value means that phone use decreased after the intervention.

The data estimate that the intervention reduces late-night phone use by approximately

\[ 1.07 \text{ to }5.33 \]

hours per week on average.

Why paired analysis is necessary

Each student's after-intervention observation is naturally matched to that same student's before-intervention observation.

The observations within a student's pair are therefore not independent.

A paired \(t\)-test analyzes the single variable

\[ d_i=\text{after}_i-\text{before}_i, \]

which correctly accounts for the pairing.

Assumptions

The important normality assumption concerns the distribution of the differences

\[ d_i, \]

not the separate before and after distributions.

Because \(n=16\) is small, severe skewness or outliers among the paired differences could substantially affect the validity of the \(t\)-procedure.

Common Pitfalls

  • Using an independent two-sample \(t\)-test.
  • Checking normality separately for the before and after samples instead of for the differences.
  • Forgetting how the difference was defined and therefore reversing the interpretation.
  • Reporting \(p<0.05\) without explaining the estimated size of the effect.

Problem 5 — Critical Evaluation of an A/B Study

A university develops a mobile app intended to increase the proportion of students who submit assignments before the deadline.

Among students who voluntarily enrolled in the app study, \(1000\) students were randomly assigned individually to one of two app versions:

  • Reminder version: \(310\) of \(500\) students submitted the assignment early.
  • Standard version: \(285\) of \(500\) students submitted the assignment early.

Researchers want to know whether the reminder version changes the probability of early submission.

Use

\[ \alpha=0.05. \]

The university considers an improvement of at least \(8\) percentage points to be large enough to justify implementing the reminder system campus-wide.

Questions

  1. Perform an appropriate hypothesis test.
  2. Construct a \(95\%\) confidence interval for the difference in population proportions.
  3. Compare statistical and practical significance.
  4. Evaluate the assumptions of the test.
  5. Discuss whether the conclusion can be generalized to all university students.
  6. Propose an improved follow-up study and justify the inferential method you would use.

Solution

Let

\[ p_R=\text{true early-submission probability under the reminder version} \]

and

\[ p_S=\text{true early-submission probability under the standard version}. \]

The sample proportions are

\[ \hat p_R=\frac{310}{500}=0.62 \]

and

\[ \hat p_S=\frac{285}{500}=0.57. \]

The observed difference is

\[ \hat p_R-\hat p_S = 0.62-0.57 = 0.05. \]

Thus, the reminder group had an observed early-submission rate \(5\) percentage points higher.

Step 1: Hypotheses

\[ H_0:p_R-p_S=0 \]

\[ H_a:p_R-p_S\neq0. \]

Step 2: Check large-sample conditions

For the reminder group,

\[ 310\text{ successes},\qquad190\text{ failures}. \]

For the standard group,

\[ 285\text{ successes},\qquad215\text{ failures}. \]

All counts are much larger than \(10\), so the normal approximation is appropriate.

Step 3: Pooled proportion

Under \(H_0\),

\[ p_R=p_S, \]

so the pooled estimate is

\[ \hat p= \frac{310+285}{500+500} = \frac{595}{1000} = 0.595. \]

Step 4: Null standard error

\[ SE_0= \sqrt{ \hat p(1-\hat p) \left( \frac1{500}+\frac1{500} \right) }. \]

Thus,

\[ SE_0= \sqrt{ 0.595(0.405)(0.004) }. \]

Therefore,

\[ SE_0\approx0.03105. \]

Step 5: Test statistic

\[ z= \frac{0.62-0.57}{0.03105} \approx1.610. \]

For a two-tailed test with \(\alpha=0.05\),

\[ z^*=\pm1.96. \]

Because

\[ |1.610|<1.96, \]

we fail to reject \(H_0\).

The p-value is approximately

\[ p\approx0.1073. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\text{The observed difference is not statistically significant at }\alpha=0.05.} \]

The data do not provide sufficiently strong evidence that the two app versions produce different early-submission probabilities.

Step 6: \(95\%\) confidence interval

For the confidence interval, do not pool the proportions.

The standard error is

\[ SE= \sqrt{ \frac{\hat p_R(1-\hat p_R)}{500} + \frac{\hat p_S(1-\hat p_S)}{500} }. \]

Thus,

\[ SE= \sqrt{ \frac{0.62(0.38)}{500} + \frac{0.57(0.43)}{500} }. \]

This gives approximately

\[ SE\approx0.03076. \]

The interval is

\[ 0.05\pm1.96(0.03076). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{(-0.0108,\;0.1108)}. \]

In percentage-point terms,

\[ \boxed{(-1.08,\;11.08)\text{ percentage points}}. \]

Interpretation of the confidence interval

The data are compatible with possibilities ranging from the reminder version being approximately \(1.1\) percentage points worse to approximately \(11.1\) percentage points better.

Because

\[ 0 \]

lies inside the confidence interval, the interval agrees with the failure to reject \(H_0\).

Statistical versus practical significance

The estimated effect is

\[ 5\text{ percentage points}. \]

The university's practical threshold is

\[ 8\text{ percentage points}. \]

The point estimate therefore falls below the threshold for practical importance.

However, the confidence interval extends above \(8\) percentage points:

\[ (-1.08,\;11.08). \]

Therefore, the current data are too imprecise to rule out an effect large enough to be practically important.

A correct conclusion is not that the reminder system has no effect. Rather,

\[ \boxed{\text{the study does not estimate the effect precisely enough to establish either no effect or an important positive effect.}} \]

Evaluation of assumptions

Independence within and between groups

Because students were randomly assigned individually to one of the two versions, assignment supports independence between treatment groups.

However, additional dependence could arise if students influenced one another, shared reminders, or belonged to tightly connected classes.

Large-sample condition

The success and failure counts in both groups are much greater than \(10\), so the normal approximation is well supported.

Random assignment versus random sampling

Random assignment supports causal comparison between the two app versions for the students who participated.

However, the students voluntarily enrolled in the study.

They were not described as a random sample of all university students.

Therefore, randomization provides strong internal validity but does not automatically provide broad external validity.

Can the results be generalized to all students?

Not necessarily.

Students who voluntarily join an app study may differ from other students in characteristics such as:

\[ \text{motivation, technology use, study habits, or organization}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\text{causal inference within the volunteer sample is stronger than generalization to the entire university.}} \]

Improved follow-up study

A stronger study could randomly sample students from the university enrollment list and then randomly assign sampled students to the reminder and standard versions.

This design would combine:

\[ \text{random sampling} \]

with

\[ \text{random assignment}. \]

Random sampling would improve the ability to generalize to the university population, while random assignment would support a causal comparison of the two app versions.

The primary analysis could again use a two-proportion \(z\)-test and confidence interval for

\[ p_R-p_S, \]

provided independence and large-count assumptions remain appropriate.

The investigators should also choose a sample size large enough to estimate whether the improvement reaches the practically important threshold of

\[ 8\text{ percentage points}. \]

Common Pitfalls

  • Treating failure to reject \(H_0\) as proof that the two app versions are identical.
  • Using the pooled proportion when constructing the confidence interval.
  • Assuming random assignment automatically makes the sample representative of all students.
  • Ignoring the width of the confidence interval.
  • Declaring a \(5\)-percentage-point difference important without comparing it with the university's stated practical benchmark.
  • Confusing statistical uncertainty with absence of an effect.

Summary of Major Ideas

These five problems illustrate several central principles of statistical inference.

A hypothesis test evaluates whether sample evidence is sufficiently inconsistent with a specified null hypothesis. A confidence interval instead provides a range of parameter values compatible with the observed data.

For a two-sided test at significance level

\[ \alpha=0.05, \]

the test and corresponding \(95\%\) confidence interval generally agree:

\[ \boxed{\text{reject }H_0 \iff \text{the null value is outside the }95\%\text{ confidence interval}.} \]

Students should also distinguish between

\[ \boxed{\text{statistical significance}} \]

and

\[ \boxed{\text{practical significance}}. \]

A statistically significant result may represent a very small effect, while a nonsignificant result can still be compatible with effects large enough to matter practically when the confidence interval is wide.

Finally, valid inference requires attention to assumptions such as independence, approximate normality, sufficient sample size, random sampling, random assignment, and the structure of the data.
