Foundations: Updating Beliefs with Evidence

Bayes' Rule answers a very practical question: given a piece of new evidence, how should we update what we believe? We start with a prior—our belief before seeing any evidence—and end with a posterior—our updated belief after seeing it.

Say we care about some event \(A\) (for example, “the patient has the disease”) and we observe evidence \(B\) (for example, “the test came back positive”). Bayes' Rule combines what we already believed about \(A\) with how likely the evidence \(B\) is, to produce an updated probability of \(A\):

\[\mathscr{P}(A \mid B) = \frac{\mathscr{P}(B \mid A)\,\mathscr{P}(A)}{\mathscr{P}(B)}\]

Each piece has a plain-language name:

  • \(\mathscr{P}(A)\) — the prior: how likely \(A\) was before any evidence.
  • \(\mathscr{P}(B \mid A)\) — the likelihood: how likely the evidence is, assuming \(A\) is true.
  • \(\mathscr{P}(B)\) — the overall chance of seeing the evidence at all, whether or not \(A\) is true.
  • \(\mathscr{P}(A \mid B)\) — the posterior: our updated belief about \(A\) once the evidence is known.

The five problems below apply this one formula to five very different situations—medicine, email, weather, manufacturing, and forensics—to show how a rare prior can make even strong-looking evidence far less conclusive than it first appears.

Problem 1 — Medical Diagnostic Testing (The Rare Disease Paradox)

Scenario: A patient is screened for a rare neurological condition. Let Event \(D\) be the case that the patient has the disease, and Event \(T\) be the case that the test result is positive.

  • Prior Probability: The disease affects 0.1% of the population: \(\mathscr{P}(D) = 0.001\).
  • Sensitivity: The test correctly identifies the disease 99% of the time: \(\mathscr{P}(T|D) = 0.99\).
  • Specificity: The test has a specificity of 95%. Consequently, the False Positive Rate—the probability of testing positive given that the patient is healthy—is calculated as the complement of specificity: \(\mathscr{P}(T|D^c) = 1 - 0.95 = 0.05\).

Instructor Solution

To determine the posterior probability that the patient has the disease given a positive test result, \(\mathscr{P}(D|T)\), we apply the Law of Total Probability to define the denominator \(\mathscr{P}(T)\):

\[\mathscr{P}(T) = \mathscr{P}(T \cap D) + \mathscr{P}(T \cap D^c) = \mathscr{P}(T|D)\mathscr{P}(D) + \mathscr{P}(T|D^c)\mathscr{P}(D^c)\]

\[\mathscr{P}(T) = (0.99 \times 0.001) + (0.05 \times 0.999) = 0.00099 + 0.04995 = 0.05094\]

Applying Bayes' Rule:

\[\mathscr{P}(D|T) = \frac{\mathscr{P}(T|D)\mathscr{P}(D)}{\mathscr{P}(T)} = \frac{0.00099}{0.05094} \approx 0.0194\]

Interpretation

Despite the test's high sensitivity, a positive result implies only a 1.94% chance of actual disease. This paradox arises because the extremely low prior \(\mathscr{P}(D)\) ensures that the absolute number of false positives from the healthy majority vastly exceeds the true positives from the diseased minority.

This logic of isolating signals from overwhelming noise extends from medicine to the digital domain, such as the algorithmic identification of unwanted communication.

Problem 2 — Email Spam Filtering (Linguistic Evidence)

Scenario: An automated filter scans emails for the presence of the trigger word “WINNER” (\(W\)) to identify spam (\(S\)).

  • Prior: 40% of the local email traffic is classified as spam: \(\mathscr{P}(S) = 0.4\).
  • Conditional (Spam): The word \(W\) appears in 60% of spam messages: \(\mathscr{P}(W|S) = 0.6\).
  • Conditional (Legitimate): The word \(W\) appears in 1% of legitimate mail: \(\mathscr{P}(W|S^c) = 0.01\).

Instructor Solution

We update the probability of spam based on the evidence of word \(W\):

\[\mathscr{P}(S|W) = \frac{\mathscr{P}(W|S)\mathscr{P}(S)}{\mathscr{P}(W)}\]

First, compute the total probability of observing the word:

\[\mathscr{P}(W) = \mathscr{P}(W|S)\mathscr{P}(S) + \mathscr{P}(W|S^c)\mathscr{P}(S^c) = (0.6 \times 0.4) + (0.01 \times 0.6) = 0.24 + 0.006 = 0.246\]

Now, solve for the posterior:

\[\mathscr{P}(S|W) = \frac{0.24}{0.246} \approx 0.9756\]

Interpretation

Specific linguistic evidence creates a massive shift in probability. The presence of the trigger word narrows the uncertainty, causing the probability of spam to jump from a 40% base rate to over 97.5%.

While digital filters operate on discrete linguistic strings, environmental forecasting requires applying the same probabilistic restriction to the continuous Borel fields of physical data.

Problem 3 — Weather Forecasting (Updating the Outlook)

Scenario: A meteorologist adjusts a rain forecast (\(R\)) based on a barometer reading of low pressure (\(B\)).

  • Prior: Historical data for the current season suggests a 20% chance of rain: \(\mathscr{P}(R) = 0.2\).
  • Conditional: When rain occurs, the barometer shows low pressure 80% of the time: \(\mathscr{P}(B|R) = 0.8\).
  • False Indicator: On dry days (\(R^c\)), the barometer mistakenly indicates low pressure 25% of the time: \(\mathscr{P}(B|R^c) = 0.25\).

Instructor Solution

Utilizing the notation for set intersections (\(\cap\)) to represent the joint occurrence of rain and low pressure:

\[\mathscr{P}(R|B) = \frac{\mathscr{P}(R \cap B)}{\mathscr{P}(B)} = \frac{\mathscr{P}(B|R)\mathscr{P}(R)}{\mathscr{P}(B|R)\mathscr{P}(R) + \mathscr{P}(B|R^c)\mathscr{P}(R^c)}\]

\[\mathscr{P}(R|B) = \frac{0.8 \times 0.2}{(0.8 \times 0.2) + (0.25 \times 0.8)} = \frac{0.16}{0.16 + 0.2} = \frac{0.16}{0.36} \approx 0.4444\]

Interpretation

Physical evidence acts as a mathematical filter on historical frequency. Although the barometer is a strong indicator, the low prior probability of rain keeps the posterior at 44.4%, demonstrating that low pressure is not a guarantee of precipitation when the baseline climate is dry.

We progress from the macro-scale volatility of environmental Borel fields to the micro-scale precision of industrial quality control.

Problem 4 — Industrial Quality Control (Defect Detection)

Scenario: A manufacturing line utilizes a sensor to flag defective items. Let \(D\) be the event of a defective item and \(F\) be the event the sensor flags the item.

  • Base Rate: 2% of the total output is defective: \(\mathscr{P}(D) = 0.02\).
  • Accuracy: The sensor has a 1% False Negative Rate: \(\mathscr{P}(F^c|D) = 0.01\), implying \(\mathscr{P}(F|D) = 0.99\).
  • False Alarm Rate: The sensor flags a functional item 3% of the time: \(\mathscr{P}(F|D^c) = 0.03\).

Instructor Solution

We seek the probability that a flagged item is actually defective, \(\mathscr{P}(D|F)\). The denominator \(\mathscr{P}(F)\) is expanded as:

\[\mathscr{P}(F) = \mathscr{P}(F \cap D) + \mathscr{P}(F \cap D^c) = (0.99 \times 0.02) + (0.03 \times 0.98) = 0.0198 + 0.0294 = 0.0492\]

Calculate the posterior probability:

\[\mathscr{P}(D|F) = \frac{0.0198}{0.0492} \approx 0.4024\]

Interpretation

The sensor's high reliability at catching defects is undermined by the high volume of functional items. Despite a 99% true-positive rate, a flag only indicates a 40.2% chance of a defect, as the majority of flags are false alarms generated by the healthy population.

The culmination of this logic is found in forensic science, where the measure of a human life is balanced against the infinitesimal match probabilities of genetic sequences.

Problem 5 — Forensic DNA Analysis (The Prosecutor's Fallacy)

Scenario: A DNA match (\(M\)) is discovered between a suspect and a sample from a crime scene.

  • Suspect Pool: The suspect is one of 10,000 individuals who had access to the scene. The prior probability of guilt (\(G\)) is thus \(\mathscr{P}(G) = 1/10{,}000 = 0.0001\).
  • Random Match Probability: The probability that an innocent person's DNA matches the sample is one in a thousand: \(\mathscr{P}(M|G^c) = 0.001\).
  • Guilt Match Probability: If the suspect is the source, a match is guaranteed: \(\mathscr{P}(M|G) = 1.0\).

Instructor Solution

We calculate the posterior probability of guilt given the evidence of a match, \(\mathscr{P}(G|M)\):

\[\mathscr{P}(G|M) = \frac{\mathscr{P}(M|G)\mathscr{P}(G)}{\mathscr{P}(M|G)\mathscr{P}(G) + \mathscr{P}(M|G^c)\mathscr{P}(G^c)}\]

\[\mathscr{P}(G|M) = \frac{1.0 \times 0.0001}{(1.0 \times 0.0001) + (0.001 \times 0.9999)} = \frac{0.0001}{0.0001 + 0.0009999} = \frac{0.0001}{0.0010999} \approx 0.0909\]

Interpretation

This illustrates the “Prosecutor's Fallacy”: confusing the rarity of a match given innocence (\(\mathscr{P}(M|G^c)\)) with the probability of innocence given a match (\(\mathscr{P}(G^c|M)\)). In a pool of 10,000, even a rare match only confers a 9.1% chance of guilt because the prior probability is so small.

These diverse applications—from pathology to forensics—all derive from the core axioms of measure theory, proving that mathematical rigor is the only reliable guide through the fog of uncertainty.
