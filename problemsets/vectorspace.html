Problem 1: Verifying Vector Space Axioms with Non-Standard Operations

Let \(V = \mathbb{R}^2\) be the set of all ordered pairs of real numbers \((x_1, x_2)\). Define vector addition \(\oplus\) using standard entrywise addition: \[(x_1, x_2) \oplus (y_1, y_2) = (x_1 + y_1, x_2 + y_2)\] Define scalar multiplication \(\odot\) by a non-standard rule for any real scalar \(c \in \mathbb{R}\): \[c \odot (x_1, x_2) = (c x_1, 0)\]

  1. Verify whether closure under vector addition and closure under scalar multiplication hold for \((V, \oplus, \odot)\).
  2. Test the scalar multiplicative identity axiom: \(1 \odot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}\) for all \(\mathbf{v} \in V\).
  3. Is \((V, \oplus, \odot)\) a valid vector space over \(\mathbb{R}\)? Explain your reasoning explicitly by citing any axiom that fails.

Solution

  1. Closure Properties:
    • Closure under addition: For any \(\mathbf{u} = (u_1, u_2) \in \mathbb{R}^2\) and \(\mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2) \in \mathbb{R}^2\), \(\mathbf{u} \oplus \mathbf{v} = (u_1 + v_1, u_2 + v_2) \in \mathbb{R}^2\) because real numbers are closed under addition.
    • Closure under scalar multiplication: For any real scalar \(c \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(\mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2) \in \mathbb{R}^2\), \(c \odot (v_1, v_2) = (c v_1, 0) \in \mathbb{R}^2\) since \(c v_1 \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(0 \in \mathbb{R}\).
  2. Scalar Multiplicative Identity Axiom (\(1 \odot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}\)):
    • Let \(\mathbf{v} = (x_1, x_2) \in \mathbb{R}^2\). Applying scalar multiplication with \(c = 1\): \[1 \odot (x_1, x_2) = (1 \cdot x_1, 0) = (x_1, 0)\]
    • For any vector where \(x_2 \neq 0\) (e.g., \(\mathbf{v} = (2, 5)\)), we have: \[1 \odot (2, 5) = (2, 0) \neq (2, 5)\]
    • Thus, \(1 \odot \mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{v}\) for all vectors with a nonzero second coordinate.
  3. Conclusion & Reasoning:
    • No, \((V, \oplus, \odot)\) is not a vector space.
    • Reason: It fails the multiplicative identity axiom (\(1\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}\)).

Problem 2: Subspace Verification and Closure Properties

Determine whether each of the following subsets is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^3\). Show all steps and explain your reasoning by explicitly testing the three subspace closure requirements.

  1. \(W_1 = \{(x_1, x_2, x_3) \in \mathbb{R}^3 \mid 2x_1 - x_2 + 3x_3 = 0\}\).
  2. \(W_2 = \{(x_1, x_2, x_3) \in \mathbb{R}^3 \mid x_1 \ge 0\}\).

Solution

  1. Subset \(W_1 = \{(x_1, x_2, x_3) \in \mathbb{R}^3 \mid 2x_1 - x_2 + 3x_3 = 0\}\):
    • Zero Vector: \(\mathbf{0} = (0, 0, 0)\) satisfies \(2(0) - (0) + 3(0) = 0\), so \(\mathbf{0} \in W_1\).
    • Closure under Addition: Let \(\mathbf{u} = (u_1, u_2, u_3) \in W_1\) and \(\mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2, v_3) \in W_1\), meaning \(2u_1 - u_2 + 3u_3 = 0\) and \(2v_1 - v_2 + 3v_3 = 0\). For \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = (u_1+v_1, u_2+v_2, u_3+v_3)\): \[2(u_1+v_1) - (u_2+v_2) + 3(u_3+v_3) = (2u_1 - u_2 + 3u_3) + (2v_1 - v_2 + 3v_3) = 0 + 0 = 0\] Thus, \(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \in W_1\).
    • Closure under Scalar Multiplication: For any scalar \(k \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(\mathbf{u} \in W_1\): \[2(k u_1) - (k u_2) + 3(k u_3) = k (2u_1 - u_2 + 3u_3) = k(0) = 0\] Thus, \(k\mathbf{u} \in W_1\).
    • Conclusion: \(W_1\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) (a plane passing through the origin).
  2. Subset \(W_2 = \{(x_1, x_2, x_3) \in \mathbb{R}^3 \mid x_1 \ge 0\}\):
    • Zero Vector: \(\mathbf{0} = (0, 0, 0) \in W_2\) because \(0 \ge 0\).
    • Closure under Scalar Multiplication: Let \(\mathbf{u} = (2, 1, 1) \in W_2\) since \(x_1 = 2 \ge 0\). Choose scalar \(c = -1 \in \mathbb{R}\): \[c\mathbf{u} = -1(2, 1, 1) = (-2, -1, -1)\] Here the first entry is \(-2 < 0\), so \(c\mathbf{u} \notin W_2\).
    • Conclusion: \(W_2\) is not a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) because it fails closure under scalar multiplication when scaled by negative numbers.

Problem 3: Linear Combinations and Computing Spans in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)

Consider the following vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\): \[\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{v}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\]

  1. Determine if the target vector \(\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 7 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\) lies in \(\text{Span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3\}\). Set up the appropriate vector equation, perform matrix row reduction, and state the required linear combination if one exists.
  2. Do the vectors \(\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3\}\) span all of \(\mathbb{R}^3\)? Explain algebraically (using pivot positions) and geometrically.

Solution

  1. Testing if \(\mathbf{b} \in \text{Span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3\}\):
    • Set up \(c_1 \mathbf{v}_1 + c_2 \mathbf{v}_2 + c_3 \mathbf{v}_3 = \mathbf{b}\): \[c_1 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + c_2 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + c_3 \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 7 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\]
    • Form the augmented matrix and row-reduce: \[\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 3 & \mid & 5 \\ 2 & 1 & 4 & \mid & 7 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 & \mid & 3 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 3 & \mid & 5 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 & \mid & -3 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 & \mid & 3 \end{bmatrix}\] \[\xrightarrow{R_2 \to -R_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 3 & \mid & 5 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 & \mid & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 & \mid & 3 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_3 \to R_3 - R_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 3 & \mid & 5 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 & \mid & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \mid & 0 \end{bmatrix}\] \[\xrightarrow{R_1 \to R_1 - R_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 & \mid & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 & \mid & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \mid & 0 \end{bmatrix}\]
    • The system is consistent. Setting \(c_3 = 0\) yields \(c_1 = 2\) and \(c_2 = 3\).
    • Verification: \(2\mathbf{v}_1 + 3\mathbf{v}_2 + 0\mathbf{v}_3 = 2(1, 2, 0) + 3(1, 1, 1) = (5, 7, 3) = \mathbf{b}\).
    • Conclusion: Yes, \(\mathbf{b}\) lies in \(\text{Span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3\}\).
  2. Spanning \(\mathbb{R}^3\):
    • No, the vectors do not span \(\mathbb{R}^3\).
    • Algebraic Reason: The echelon form of the matrix has only 2 pivot positions (rows 1 and 2), meaning \(A\) does not have a pivot in every row.
    • Geometric Reason: Because there are only 2 linearly independent vectors in the set, \(\text{Span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3\}\) forms a 2-dimensional plane passing through the origin in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), rather than filling all of 3-space.

Problem 4: Assessing Linear Independence and Dependence Relations

Consider the set of vectors \(S = \{\mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{u}_2, \mathbf{u}_3\} \subset \mathbb{R}^3\), where: \[\mathbf{u}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{u}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{u}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}\]

  1. Write down the vector equation \(c_1 \mathbf{u}_1 + c_2 \mathbf{u}_2 + c_3 \mathbf{u}_3 = \mathbf{0}\) for linear independence.
  2. Convert the vector equation into an augmented matrix and row-reduce it to reduced row echelon form (RREF).
  3. State whether the set \(S\) is linearly independent or linearly dependent. If dependent, find an explicit linear dependence relation.

Solution

  1. Vector Equation: \[c_1 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + c_2 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + c_3 \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\]
  2. Row Reduction: \[\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 5 & \mid & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 1 & \mid & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 5 & \mid & 0 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{\substack{R_2 \to R_2 + R_1 \\ R_3 \to R_3 - 2R_1}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 5 & \mid & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 6 & \mid & 0 \\ 0 & -3 & -5 & \mid & 0 \end{bmatrix}\] \[\xrightarrow{R_2 \to \frac{1}{3}R_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 5 & \mid & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 & \mid & 0 \\ 0 & -3 & -5 & \mid & 0 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_3 \to R_3 + 3R_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 5 & \mid & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 & \mid & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & \mid & 0 \end{bmatrix}\] \[\xrightarrow{\substack{R_1 \to R_1 - 5R_3 \\ R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_3}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & \mid & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & \mid & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & \mid & 0 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_1 \to R_1 - 2R_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & \mid & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & \mid & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & \mid & 0 \end{bmatrix}\]
  3. Conclusion & Dependence Relation:
    • Every column contains a pivot, meaning there are no free variables.
    • The only solution is the trivial solution \(c_1 = 0, c_2 = 0, c_3 = 0\).
    • Therefore, \(S = \{\mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{u}_2, \mathbf{u}_3\}\) is linearly independent. (There is no nontrivial linear dependence relation.)

Problem 5: Basis and Dimension of a Subspace Defined by Equations

Let \(W \subset \mathbb{R}^4\) be the solution set (null space) of the homogeneous system of linear equations: \[\begin{aligned} x_1 + 2x_2 - 3x_3 + x_4 &= 0 \\ 2x_1 + 4x_2 - 5x_3 + 3x_4 &= 0 \end{aligned}\]

  1. Form the coefficient matrix \(A\) for this system and reduce it to reduced row echelon form (RREF).
  2. Identify the basic (pivot) variables and the free variables.
  3. Write the general solution in parametric vector form to find a set of basis vectors for \(W\).
  4. State the dimension \(\dim(W)\) and explain why your set of vectors constitutes a valid basis.

Solution

  1. Coefficient Matrix and RREF: \[A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & -3 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 & -5 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\] Row-reduce \(A\): \[A \xrightarrow{R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & -3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_1 \to R_1 + 3R_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\]
  2. Pivots and Free Variables:
    • Pivot columns: Column 1 (\(x_1\)) and Column 3 (\(x_3\)).
    • Free variables: \(x_2\) and \(x_4\).
  3. Parametric Form and Basis: Write basic variables in terms of free variables: \[x_1 = -2x_2 - 4x_4\] \[x_3 = -x_4\] Express the solution vector \(\mathbf{x} \in W\) in parametric vector form: \[\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \\ x_4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2x_2 - 4x_4 \\ x_2 \\ -x_4 \\ x_4 \end{bmatrix} = x_2 \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + x_4 \begin{bmatrix} -4 \\ 0 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\]
    • Basis for \(W\): \[\mathcal{B} = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} -4 \\ 0 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}\]
  4. Dimension & Explanation:
    • Dimension: \(\dim(W) = 2\).
    • Explanation: The solution space \(W\) is spanned by \(\mathcal{B}\), and the vectors in \(\mathcal{B}\) are linearly independent because each contains a 1 in a position where the other contains a 0 (corresponding to the free variables \(x_2\) and \(x_4\)). Thus, \(\mathcal{B}\) satisfies both requirements for a basis.
