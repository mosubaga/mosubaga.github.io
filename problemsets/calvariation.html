Foundations: Optimizing Over Curves, Not Points

Ordinary calculus finds the point \(x\) that minimizes a function \(f(x)\). The calculus of variations asks a more ambitious question: among all admissible curves \(y(x)\) connecting two states, which one minimizes (or maximizes) a functional—a rule that assigns a single number to an entire curve? A functional typically has the form

\[J[y] = \int_a^b F\bigl(x, y(x), y'(x)\bigr)\, dx.\]

The candidate that extremizes \(J\) must satisfy the Euler–Lagrange equation:

\[\frac{\partial F}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'}\right) = 0.\]

When an endpoint is free rather than fixed, the first variation must vanish there as well, producing a natural (transversality) boundary condition of the form \(\partial F/\partial y' = 0\) at that endpoint. When the admissible curves must also satisfy an integral constraint \(\int_a^b G(x,y,y')\,dx = k\), we adjoin the constraint with a Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda\) and instead extremize the augmented functional \(\int_a^b (F - \lambda G)\,dx\), applying the Euler–Lagrange equation to the combined integrand.

Each problem below follows the same three-step outline: (a) formulate the functional (and any constraint), (b) derive the extremal curve from the Euler–Lagrange equation, and (c) analyze the boundary conditions—fixed, natural, or constraint-determined—that pin down the constants of integration.

Problem 1 — Shortest Path to a Moving Target (Natural Boundary Conditions)

Scenario: Find the curve \(y(x)\) of shortest length that starts at the fixed point \((0,0)\) and ends anywhere on the vertical line \(x=b\); that is, \(y(b)\) is free.

  • Functional to minimize: arc length \(\displaystyle L[y] = \int_0^b \sqrt{1+y'^2}\, dx\).
  • Fixed endpoint: \(y(0) = 0\).
  • Free endpoint: \(y(b)\) unconstrained.

Instructor Solution

(a) Functional formulation. Here \(F(x,y,y') = \sqrt{1+y'^2}\), which depends on neither \(x\) nor \(y\) explicitly.

(b) Variational derivation. Since \(\partial F/\partial y = 0\), the Euler–Lagrange equation reduces to

\[\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'}\right) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} = \frac{y'}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}} = m,\]

a constant. Solving for \(y'\) gives \(y' = \text{const}\), so the extremal is a straight line \(y = mx + c\). The fixed condition \(y(0)=0\) forces \(c=0\), leaving \(y = mx\).

(c) Boundary condition analysis. Because \(y(b)\) is free, the first variation contributes a boundary term \(\left.\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial y'}\right|_{x=b}\delta y(b)\), which must vanish for arbitrary \(\delta y(b)\). This yields the natural boundary condition

\[\left.\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'}\right|_{x=b} = \frac{m}{\sqrt{1+m^2}} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m = 0.\]

Hence \(y(x) \equiv 0\): the shortest path is the horizontal segment from \((0,0)\) to \((b,0)\), which meets the vertical target line \(x=b\) at a right angle.

Interpretation

The natural boundary condition recovers a classical fact of geometry: the shortest connector from a point to a line meets that line orthogonally. This transversality principle is the same mechanism that governs how light paths meet a mirror or interface in geometric optics.

A free endpoint on a line was the simplest case; next we fix both endpoints but let the curve sweep out a surface, trading a boundary-condition subtlety for a genuinely nonlinear Euler–Lagrange equation.

Problem 2 — Minimal Surface of Revolution (The Catenoid)

Scenario: A curve \(y(x) > 0\) joins two fixed points \((a, y_a)\) and \((b, y_b)\), \(a

  • Functional to minimize: \(\displaystyle S[y] = 2\pi\int_a^b y\sqrt{1+y'^2}\, dx\).
  • Fixed endpoints: \(y(a) = y_a\), \(y(b) = y_b\).

Instructor Solution

(a) Functional formulation. Drop the constant \(2\pi\) and take \(F(y,y') = y\sqrt{1+y'^2}\), which has no explicit \(x\)-dependence.

(b) Variational derivation. Since \(F\) is \(x\)-independent, the Beltrami identity \(F - y'\,\partial F/\partial y' = C\) applies:

\[\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} = \frac{y\,y'}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}, \qquad F - y'\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} = \frac{y(1+y'^2) - y\,y'^2}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}} = \frac{y}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}} = C.\]

Solving for \(y'\): \(y' = \sqrt{(y/C)^2 - 1}\), which separates as \(\dfrac{dy}{\sqrt{(y/C)^2-1}} = dx\). Integrating gives \(C\cosh^{-1}(y/C) = x - x_0\), i.e.

\[y(x) = C\cosh\!\left(\frac{x-x_0}{C}\right),\]

a catenoid profile.

(c) Boundary condition analysis. Both endpoints are fixed (Dirichlet conditions), so no natural boundary condition arises; instead, the two constants \(C\) and \(x_0\) are determined by solving \(y_a = C\cosh\!\big((a-x_0)/C\big)\) and \(y_b = C\cosh\!\big((b-x_0)/C\big)\) simultaneously. For endpoints spread far enough apart, no real solution exists and the area-minimizing configuration degenerates to two disks plus the axis (the Goldschmidt discontinuous solution)—a reminder that a smooth Euler–Lagrange solution is not always the global minimizer.

Interpretation

A soap film spanning two coaxial rings minimizes surface energy, which is proportional to area; the resulting equilibrium shape is exactly this catenoid, the same curve as a hanging chain rotated about its axis of symmetry.

Minimizing area gave a static equilibrium shape; the next problem minimizes time itself, for a bead sliding under gravity.

Problem 3 — The Brachistochrone (Fastest Descent Under Gravity)

Scenario: A frictionless bead starts from rest at the origin and slides under uniform gravity \(g\) along a wire shaped as \(y(x)\) (with \(y\) measured positive downward) to a fixed lower point \((x_1, y_1)\). Find the shape that minimizes the total descent time.

  • Functional to minimize: using \(v = \sqrt{2gy}\) from energy conservation, \(\displaystyle T[y] = \int_0^{x_1} \frac{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}{\sqrt{2gy}}\, dx\).
  • Fixed endpoints: \(y(0)=0\), \(y(x_1) = y_1\).

Instructor Solution

(a) Functional formulation. \(F(y,y') = \sqrt{1+y'^2}/\sqrt{2gy}\) again has no explicit \(x\)-dependence, so the Beltrami identity applies.

(b) Variational derivation. Compute

\[\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} = \frac{y'}{\sqrt{2gy}\sqrt{1+y'^2}}, \qquad F - y'\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} = \frac{(1+y'^2) - y'^2}{\sqrt{2gy}\sqrt{1+y'^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2gy}\sqrt{1+y'^2}} = C.\]

Rearranging, \(y(1+y'^2) = \dfrac{1}{2gC^2} \equiv 2a\), a constant. This first-order ODE is solved by the parametrization

\[x(\theta) = a(\theta - \sin\theta), \qquad y(\theta) = a(1-\cos\theta),\]

which traces a cycloid, as direct substitution confirms: \(dx = a(1-\cos\theta)\,d\theta\), \(dy = a\sin\theta\,d\theta\), so \(y'=dy/dx = \sin\theta/(1-\cos\theta)\), and \(y(1+y'^2) = a(1-\cos\theta)\cdot\dfrac{(1-\cos\theta)^2+\sin^2\theta}{(1-\cos\theta)^2} = 2a\), independent of \(\theta\). ✓

(c) Boundary condition analysis. Both endpoints are fixed, so no natural boundary condition appears; the scale \(a\) and the parameter range \(0 \le \theta \le \theta_1\) are fixed by requiring the cycloid to pass through \((x_1,y_1)\).

Interpretation

This is the classical brachistochrone problem posed by Johann Bernoulli in 1696: the curve of fastest descent is not the straight line but an inverted cycloid, because a steeper initial drop builds speed quickly enough to offset the longer path length.

The brachistochrone minimized an unconstrained functional; the remaining two problems introduce an explicit constraint, requiring a Lagrange multiplier to adjoin it to the Euler–Lagrange machinery.

Problem 4 — The Isoperimetric Problem (Dido's Problem)

Scenario: Among all curves \(y(x)\) with \(y(-a) = y(a) = 0\) and a fixed arc length \(L > 2a\), find the curve that encloses the maximum area with the segment of the \(x\)-axis between \(-a\) and \(a\).

  • Functional to maximize: \(\displaystyle A[y] = \int_{-a}^{a} y\, dx\).
  • Constraint: \(\displaystyle \int_{-a}^{a} \sqrt{1+y'^2}\, dx = L\).
  • Fixed endpoints: \(y(\pm a) = 0\).

Instructor Solution

(a) Functional formulation. Adjoin the constraint with a Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda\) and extremize \(J[y] = \int_{-a}^{a}\bigl(y - \lambda\sqrt{1+y'^2}\bigr)\,dx\), so \(F = y - \lambda\sqrt{1+y'^2}\).

(b) Variational derivation. Since \(\partial F/\partial y = 1\) and \(\partial F/\partial y' = -\lambda y'/\sqrt{1+y'^2}\), the Euler–Lagrange equation gives

\[1 - \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{-\lambda y'}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}\right) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\lambda y'}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}\right) = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\lambda y'}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}} = x + C_1.\]

By the left–right symmetry of the boundary data (\(y(-a)=y(a)=0\)), the extremal is symmetric about \(x=0\), so \(C_1 = 0\). Squaring, \(\lambda^2 y'^2 = x^2(1+y'^2)\), so

\[y' = \frac{x}{\sqrt{\lambda^2 - x^2}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y(x) = C_2 - \sqrt{\lambda^2-x^2}.\]

Rearranged, \(x^2 + (y - C_2)^2 = \lambda^2\): the extremal is a circular arc of radius \(\lambda\).

(c) Boundary condition analysis. Both endpoints are fixed, so \(y(\pm a)=0\) pins \(C_2 = \sqrt{\lambda^2-a^2}\), and the multiplier \(\lambda\) itself is fixed not by a boundary condition but by the constraint equation—substituting the arc back into \(\int\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx = L\) relates \(\lambda\) to \(L\) and \(a\) through the subtended angle \(L = 2\lambda\arcsin(a/\lambda)\).

Interpretation

This is Dido's problem: of all curves of a given length anchored at two fixed points, the circular arc encloses the greatest area—the variational proof of the isoperimetric inequality in the plane.

Dido's problem constrained total length while maximizing area; the final problem constrains length while minimizing potential energy, yielding the shape of every hanging cable and power line.

Problem 5 — The Catenary (Shape of a Hanging Cable)

Scenario: A flexible, inextensible cable of fixed length \(L\) and linear density \(\rho\) hangs under gravity \(g\) between two fixed supports at \((-a,0)\) and \((a,0)\). Find the curve \(y(x)\) it settles into—the shape minimizing gravitational potential energy subject to the fixed-length constraint.

  • Functional to minimize: \(\displaystyle U[y] = \rho g\int_{-a}^{a} y\sqrt{1+y'^2}\, dx\).
  • Constraint: \(\displaystyle \int_{-a}^{a} \sqrt{1+y'^2}\, dx = L\).
  • Fixed endpoints: \(y(\pm a) = 0\).

Instructor Solution

(a) Functional formulation. Adjoin the length constraint with multiplier \(\lambda\) (absorbing \(\rho g\) into a reduced multiplier \(\mu = \lambda/(\rho g)\)) to form \(F = (y-\mu)\sqrt{1+y'^2}\), the same structural form as Problem 2 with \(y\) shifted by \(\mu\).

(b) Variational derivation. \(F\) has no explicit \(x\)-dependence, so the Beltrami identity gives

\[\frac{y-\mu}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}} = C \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y(x) - \mu = C\cosh\!\left(\frac{x-x_0}{C}\right).\]

By symmetry of the boundary data about \(x=0\), \(x_0 = 0\), giving the catenary

\[y(x) = \mu + C\cosh\!\left(\frac{x}{C}\right).\]

(c) Boundary condition analysis. Both endpoints are fixed, so no natural boundary condition is needed. The condition \(y(a)=y(-a)=0\) (automatically symmetric) gives one equation, \(\mu = -C\cosh(a/C)\), and the length constraint \(\int_{-a}^{a}\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx = 2C\sinh(a/C) = L\) supplies the second, fixing \(C\) (and hence \(\mu\)) in terms of \(a\) and \(L\).

Interpretation

Every hanging chain, cable, or power line adopts this catenary shape—not a parabola, though the two look similar—because it is precisely the configuration that minimizes gravitational potential energy while holding the cable's total length fixed.

All five extremals below descend from the same Euler–Lagrange framework; only the presence of an explicit \(x\)-dependence, a free endpoint, or a constraint changes which shortcut—direct integration, the Beltrami identity, or a Lagrange multiplier—applies.
