Problem 1: Bounded Monotonic Sequences

Problem Statement

Consider the sequence \((a_n)_{n=1}^\infty\) defined recursively by: \[a_1 = \sqrt{2}, \quad a_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + a_n} \quad \text{for all } n \ge 1\]

  1. Prove by induction that \((a_n)\) is bounded above by \(2\).
  2. Prove that \((a_n)\) is strictly monotone increasing.
  3. Deduce that the sequence converges and find its limit \(\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n\).

Solution Steps

  1. Boundedness: Establish \(a_n < 2\) for all \(n \ge 1\) using mathematical induction.
    • Base case (\(n=1\)): \(a_1 = \sqrt{2} < 2\), which holds.
    • Inductive step: Assume \(a_k < 2\) for \(k \ge 1\). Then \(a_{k+1} = \sqrt{2 + a_k} < \sqrt{2 + 2} = \sqrt{4} = 2\). By induction, \(a_n < 2\) for all \(n \ge 1\).
  2. Monotonicity: Show that \(a_{n+1} > a_n\) for all \(n \ge 1\).
    • Base case (\(n=1\)): \(a_2 = \sqrt{2 + \sqrt{2}} > \sqrt{2} = a_1\).
    • Inductive step: Assume \(a_k < a_{k+1}\). Then \(2 + a_k < 2 + a_{k+1} \implies \sqrt{2 + a_k} < \sqrt{2 + a_{k+1}} \implies a_{k+1} < a_{k+2}\). Thus, \((a_n)\) is strictly monotone increasing.
  3. Convergence and Limit:
    • Since \((a_n)\) is bounded above and monotone increasing, the Monotone Convergence Theorem guarantees that \(\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = L\) exists.
    • Taking the limit on both sides of \(a_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + a_n}\), we obtain \(L = \sqrt{2 + L} \implies L^2 - L - 2 = 0 \implies (L - 2)(L + 1) = 0\).
    • Since \(a_n \ge \sqrt{2} > 0\) for all \(n\), we must have \(L \ge \sqrt{2}\), ruling out \(L = -1\).

Answer

The sequence \((a_n)\) is monotone increasing and bounded above by \(2\), so it converges by the Monotone Convergence Theorem. Its limit is: \[\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = 2\]

Problem 2: Sums of Telescoping and Geometric Series

Problem Statement

Compute the exact sum for each of the following infinite series:

  1. \(\displaystyle \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n(n+1)(n+2)}\)
  2. \(\displaystyle \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{3 \cdot 2^n - 2 \cdot 3^{n-1}}{5^n}\)

Solution Steps

  1. Part 1 (Telescoping Series):
    • Apply partial fraction decomposition: \[\frac{1}{n(n+1)(n+2)} = \frac{1/2}{n} - \frac{1}{n+1} + \frac{1/2}{n+2} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \left(\frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{n+1}\right) - \left(\frac{1}{n+1} - \frac{1}{n+2}\right) \right)\]
    • Define \(b_n = \frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{n+1}\). The \(N\)-th partial sum is: \[s_N = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{n=1}^N (b_n - b_{n+1}) = \frac{1}{2} (b_1 - b_{N+1}) = \frac{1}{2} \left( \left(1 - \frac{1}{2}\right) - \left(\frac{1}{N+1} - \frac{1}{N+2}\right) \right)\]
    • Evaluate the limit as \(N \to \infty\): \[S = \lim_{N \to \infty} s_N = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{1}{2} - 0 \right) = \frac{1}{4}\]
  2. Part 2 (Geometric Series):
    • Split the series into two separate geometric series \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty a r^{n-1}\): \[\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{3 \cdot 2^n}{5^n} - \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{2 \cdot 3^{n-1}}{5^n} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{6}{5} \left(\frac{2}{5}\right)^{n-1} - \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{2}{5} \left(\frac{3}{5}\right)^{n-1}\]
    • Both series satisfy \(|r| < 1\) (\(r_1 = 2/5\) and \(r_2 = 3/5\)), so both converge: \[S_1 = \frac{6/5}{1 - 2/5} = \frac{6/5}{3/5} = 2, \quad S_2 = \frac{2/5}{1 - 3/5} = \frac{2/5}{2/5} = 1\]
    • The sum is \(S_1 - S_2 = 2 - 1 = 1\).

Answer

  1. \(\displaystyle \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n(n+1)(n+2)} = \frac{1}{4}\)
  2. \(\displaystyle \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{3 \cdot 2^n - 2 \cdot 3^{n-1}}{5^n} = 1\)

Problem 3: Convergence Tests for Infinite Series

Problem Statement

For each series below, determine whether it converges or diverges. Explicitly state the test or theorem applied, verify all required hypotheses, and show your work:

  1. \(\displaystyle \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{n^2 + 1}{3^n}\)
  2. \(\displaystyle \sum_{n=1}^\infty (-1)^{n+1} \frac{\ln n}{n}\)
  3. \(\displaystyle \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{\sqrt{n+1} - \sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\)

Solution Steps

  1. Series 1: Apply Cauchy's Ratio Test: \[L = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left| \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} \right| = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{(n+1)^2 + 1}{3^{n+1}} \cdot \frac{3^n}{n^2 + 1} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{n^2 + 2n + 2}{n^2 + 1} = \frac{1}{3}\] Since \(L = \frac{1}{3} < 1\), the Ratio Test guarantees that the series converges absolutely (and hence converges).
  2. Series 2: Apply the Alternating Series Test (Leibniz's Test):
    • Identify \(b_n = \frac{\ln n}{n} > 0\) for \(n \ge 2\).
    • Verify Condition (i): \(\lim_{n \to \infty} b_n = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{\ln n}{n} = 0\) (via L'Hôpital's Rule).
    • Verify Condition (ii): To show \((b_n)\) is eventually decreasing, consider \(f(x) = \frac{\ln x}{x}\) for \(x \ge 2\). Its derivative \(f'(x) = \frac{1 - \ln x}{x^2} < 0\) for \(x > e \approx 2.718\). Thus, \(b_n \ge b_{n+1}\) for all \(n \ge 3\).
    • By the Alternating Series Test, the series converges.
  3. Series 3: Rationalize the numerator and use the Limit Comparison Test: \[a_n = \frac{(\sqrt{n+1} - \sqrt{n})(\sqrt{n+1} + \sqrt{n})}{\sqrt{n}(\sqrt{n+1} + \sqrt{n})} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n^2 + n} + n}\] Compare with \(b_n = \frac{1}{n}\): \[\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{a_n}{b_n} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n}{\sqrt{n^2 + n} + n} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + 1/n} + 1} = \frac{1}{2}\] Since \(\frac{1}{2} > 0\) and \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n}\) is the divergent harmonic series (\(p\)-series with \(p=1\)), the Limit Comparison Test establishes that \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n\) diverges.

Answer

  1. Converges (by the Ratio Test, \(L = 1/3 < 1\)).
  2. Converges (by the Alternating Series Test).
  3. Diverges (by the Limit Comparison Test with the harmonic series).

Problem 4: Power Series and Interval of Convergence

Problem Statement

Determine the radius of convergence \(R\) and the exact interval of convergence \(I\) for the power series: \[\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n (x - 3)^n}{n \cdot 2^n}\] Analyze convergence at both endpoints explicitly.

Solution Steps

  1. Radius of Convergence via Ratio Test: Apply the Ratio Test for absolute convergence: \[L = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left| \frac{a_{n+1}(x)}{a_n(x)} \right| = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left| \frac{(-1)^{n+1} (x - 3)^{n+1}}{(n+1) 2^{n+1}} \cdot \frac{n \cdot 2^n}{(-1)^n (x - 3)^n} \right| = |x - 3| \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n}{2(n+1)} = \frac{|x - 3|}{2}\] The series converges absolutely when \(L < 1 \implies \frac{|x - 3|}{2} < 1 \implies |x - 3| < 2\). Therefore, the radius of convergence is \(R = 2\). The open interval of convergence is \((3 - 2, 3 + 2) = (1, 5)\).
  2. Endpoint Analysis:
    • At \(x = 5\): The power series becomes: \[\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n (5 - 3)^n}{n \cdot 2^n} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n 2^n}{n \cdot 2^n} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n}{n}\] This is the alternating harmonic series, which converges by the Alternating Series Test.
    • At \(x = 1\): The power series becomes: \[\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n (1 - 3)^n}{n \cdot 2^n} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n (-2)^n}{n \cdot 2^n} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^{2n} 2^n}{n \cdot 2^n} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n}\] This is the standard harmonic series, which diverges (\(p\)-series with \(p=1\)).

Answer

  • Radius of Convergence: \(R = 2\)
  • Interval of Convergence: \(I = (1, 5]\)

Problem 5: Critical Evaluation of Convergence Claims

Problem Statement

A student claims: "If \(\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = 0\), then the infinite series \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n\) must converge."

  1. Disprove the student's claim by providing an explicit counterexample and proving that your counterexample diverges.
  2. State the correct \(n\)-th Term Test for Divergence.
  3. Prove the \(n\)-th Term Test theorem: If \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n\) converges, then \(\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = 0\).

Solution Steps

  1. Counterexample: Consider the harmonic series \(a_n = \frac{1}{n}\).
    • The sequence of terms satisfies \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} = 0\).
    • To prove divergence, group the terms in partial sums of powers of two \(s_{2^k}\): \[s_{2^k} = 1 + \frac{1}{2} + \left(\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{4}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{5} + \dots + \frac{1}{8}\right) + \dots + \left(\frac{1}{2^{k-1}+1} + \dots + \frac{1}{2^k}\right)\] \[s_{2^k} > 1 + \frac{1}{2} + \left(\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{8} + \dots + \frac{1}{8}\right) + \dots = 1 + \frac{k}{2}\]
    • Since \(\lim_{k \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{k}{2}\right) = \infty\), the sequence of partial sums \((s_n)\) is unbounded and therefore diverges.
  2. Correct \(n\)-th Term Test for Divergence: If \(\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n \neq 0\) or if \(\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n\) does not exist, then the series \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n\) diverges.
  3. Formal Proof of Necessary Condition:
    • Let \(s_n = \sum_{k=1}^n a_k\) denote the \(n\)-th partial sum.
    • By definition of series convergence, \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n = S \implies \lim_{n \to \infty} s_n = S\).
    • Note that \(a_n = s_n - s_{n-1}\) for all \(n \ge 2\).
    • Applying the Algebraic Limit Laws for convergent sequences: \[\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = \lim_{n \to \infty} (s_n - s_{n-1}) = \lim_{n \to \infty} s_n - \lim_{n \to \infty} s_{n-1} = S - S = 0 \quad \blacksquare\]

Answer

  1. Counterexample: The harmonic series \(a_n = \frac{1}{n}\) has \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} = 0\), yet \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n}\) diverges to \(\infty\).
  2. \(n\)-th Term Test for Divergence: If \(\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n \neq 0\) (or does not exist), then \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n\) diverges.
  3. Proof: Expressing \(a_n = s_n - s_{n-1}\) yields \(\lim a_n = S - S = 0\).
